# The Covert Use of Technologies and Substances by Authorities Against Civilians

## Question

How have state authorities — intelligence agencies, military bodies, and law enforcement — covertly used technologies and substances against civilians, what is documented and verified, and what accountability frameworks exist?

## Executive Summary

1. Historical programs confirm that authorities have repeatedly subjected civilians and unwitting subjects to covert experimentation and disruption. COINTELPRO (FBI, 1956–1971), Project MKUltra (CIA, 1953–early 1970s), the Tuskegee Syphilis Study (USPHS, 1932–1972), the Guatemala syphilis experiments (1946–1948), and UK chemical/biological testing at Porton Down (1916–onward) are established through declassified records, congressional inquiries, and court findings.
2. Commercial spyware has become the dominant modern vector of covert state action against civilians. NSO Group's Pegasus — sold to governments ostensibly to fight crime and terrorism — has been forensically confirmed on the phones of journalists, activists, lawyers, and political dissidents across multiple countries.
3. Chemical and pharmacological substances have been used covertly both for experimentation and for operational targeting. Novichok nerve agents were used against Sergei Skripal (2018) and Alexei Navalny (2020); Iran allegedly used anaesthetic-laced riot-control gases against protesters (2022 and 2026).
4. Directed-energy and non-lethal weapons developed for military crowd control — the Active Denial System ("heat ray"), acoustic devices, and microwave systems — have been considered or deployed against civilian protesters, raising proportionality and health concerns.
5. Law-enforcement cell-site simulators (IMSI catchers / "Stingrays") enable dragnet surveillance of all phones in a radius without individualised warrants, with documented use in the United States, Canada, and elsewhere.
6. Accountability remains weak and inconsistent: MKUltra victims were never formally compensated; the UN Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression has called for a moratorium on commercial spyware sales; the OPCW has condemned chemical weapons use but lacks independent inspection powers over covert sites.
7. A persistent pattern emerges: technologies and substances are developed under national-security or public-order justifications, then applied against civilians — particularly dissidents, minorities, and protesters — beyond their stated lawful purpose.

## Methodology

This report draws on publicly available sources retrieved through web search: encyclopaedic entries (Wikipedia, Britannica), official government and intergovernmental sources (CDC, GOV.UK, OHCHR, OPCW, US Congress, GAO), journalism (Washington Post, CBS, PBS Frontline, Time), and specialist analysis (CSIS, Arms Control Association, FDD, ISIS, Amnesty International, EFF, Physicians for Human Rights).

Search angles covered: (a) historical covert programs (COINTELPRO, MKUltra, Tuskegee, Guatemala, Porton Down); (b) modern commercial spyware (Pegasus); (c) cell-site simulators; (d) directed-energy and non-lethal weapons; (e) state use of chemical agents (Novichok, Iran riot-control gases); (f) international accountability frameworks.

Limitations: This investigation relies on open-source material. Claims from advocacy sites that assert ongoing satellite-based harassment of individuals (e.g., surveillanceissues.com) are excluded from verified findings, as these claims lack independent corroboration by primary or mainstream sources and are associated with unverified reports. The report distinguishes verified historical fact from contested or disputed contemporary cases.

## Findings

### 1. Historical Covert Programs Against Civilians

#### COINTELPRO (FBI, 1956–1971)

COINTELPRO was a series of covert FBI projects aimed at surveilling, infiltrating, discrediting, and disrupting domestic political organisations deemed subversive. Targets included civil rights leaders (Martin Luther King Jr.), the Communist Party USA, anti-Vietnam War organisers, the Black Panther Party, feminist organisations, and others. Tactics included intense surveillance, organisational infiltration, anonymous mailings, forged documents, psychological warfare, false criminal charges, and police harassment. A US Senate investigation concluded the techniques "would be intolerable in a democratic society" and that the Bureau conducted "a sophisticated vigilante operation aimed squarely at preventing the exercise of First Amendment rights." The program was exposed in 1971 after activists burglarised an FBI office in Media, Pennsylvania, and released files to the press.

#### Project MKUltra (CIA, 1953–early 1970s)

MKUltra was an illegal CIA human experimentation program to develop procedures and drugs for altering human behaviour, including mind-control and interrogation techniques. It involved covert administration of psychotropic drugs (notably LSD) to unwitting subjects. The program never achieved its operational objectives and was terminated in the early 1970s due to low reproducibility, methodological deficiencies, ethical objections over torture and covert practices, and the destruction of most records. The 1975 Church Committee and Rockefeller Commission established the facts through surviving records. No MKUltra victim was ever formally compensated by the US government, despite court cases; governments invoked sovereign immunity and evidentiary gaps.

#### Tuskegee Syphilis Study and Guatemala Experiments

The Tuskegee Study (1932–1972), run by the US Public Health Service, observed untreated syphilis in approximately 400 African American men who were deceived about the nature of their treatment. The Guatemala syphilis experiments (1946–1948), led by John Charles Cutler (who also participated in Tuskegee), deliberately infected roughly 1,300 people — soldiers, sex workers, orphans, prisoners, and mental-hospital inmates — with syphilis, gonorrhea, and other diseases. Both programs are established through official acknowledgement; the CDC now documents Tuskegee as a case study, and President Clinton formally apologised in 1997.

#### Porton Down and UK Chemical/Biological Testing

Porton Down, the UK's chemical and biological research facility (opened 1916), conducted extensive testing of chemical agents (mustard gas, lewisite, nitrogen mustard, sarin) on military volunteers and, in some cases, on civilian populations without informed consent. The Dorset Biological Warfare Experiments (1953–1975) dispersed zinc cadmium sulfide as a simulated biological agent over populated areas of the UK to test dispersal patterns. The Rawalpindi experiments tested mustard gas on hundreds of British Indian Army soldiers before and during WWII. One serviceman, Ronald Maddison, died in a 1953 sarin trial; an inquest in 2004 returned a verdict of unlawful killing.

### 2. Commercial Spyware: Pegasus

Pegasus is spyware developed by Israel's NSO Group, designed to be covertly and remotely installed on iOS and Android phones, granting full access to data, camera, and microphone. Although marketed for fighting crime and terrorism, the 2021 Pegasus Project investigation — coordinated by Forbidden Stories with technical support from Amnesty International's Security Lab, involving 17 news organisations — revealed that governments worldwide used Pegasus to surveil journalists, lawyers, human rights activists, and political dissidents. A leaked list of 50,000 phone numbers included opposition politicians, activists, and journalists. Targets included individuals close to murdered Saudi journalist Jamal Khashoggi. In India, Amnesty confirmed repeated use of Pegasus against high-profile journalists. NSO has denied misuse but a US court in 2024 ordered NSO to hand over Pegasus code to WhatsApp in an ongoing lawsuit.

### 3. Cell-Site Simulators (Stingrays / IMSI Catchers)

Stingray devices, manufactured by Harris Corporation, impersonate legitimate cell towers, tricking all phones within a radius into connecting, enabling capture of identifying numbers, location data, and in some configurations call and text content. Initially developed for military and intelligence use, they have been in widespread use by US state and local law enforcement since at least the 1990s. The Electronic Frontier Foundation and civil-liberties groups have documented concerns that these devices enable dragnet surveillance without individualised warrants, sweeping in bystanders' data.

### 4. Directed-Energy and Non-Lateral Weapons

The Active Denial System (ADS), a US military directed-energy weapon nicknamed the "heat ray," uses millimetre-wavelength electromagnetic rays to heat skin on contact, causing a painful burning sensation; it was developed for area denial, perimeter security, and crowd control. The Long Range Acoustic Device ("sound cannon") has been used by law enforcement to disperse crowds. Physicians for Human Rights and the ACLU have documented health impacts of directed-energy devices and concerns about their use against protesters. A whistleblower disclosed that US federal officials considered using a heat-ray device against protesters in Washington, DC. The MEDUSA weapon exploits the microwave auditory effect. Havana syndrome — unexplained health incidents among US personnel — was originally suspected to involve microwave weapons; a 2022 CIA interim assessment found it was not the result of a sustained global campaign by a hostile power, though a 2024 CBS/60 Minutes investigation found links to Russian intelligence and over 65 victims believing they were targeted, and the Washington Post reported a CIA investigation into a Norwegian pulsed-energy experiment.

### 5. Chemical Agents: Novichok and Riot-Control Gases

Novichok is a family of nerve agents developed by the Soviet Union/Russia (1971–1993) as part of the secret Foliant program. Novichok was used in the attempted assassination of Sergei Skripal in Salisbury, UK (2018), killing one unrelated civilian, and in the poisoning of Alexei Navalny (2020). The OPCW confirmed a Novichok-type cholinesterase inhibitor in Navalny's blood and urine. Russia denied involvement. In November 2019 the OPCW added Novichok agents to the CWC's controlled-substances list. Separately, during Iran's 2022 Mahsa Amini protests and an alleged 2026 crackdown, protesters reported that riot-control gases induced anaesthesia inconsistent with traditional CS gas, associated with fentanyl and medetomidine; Iranian-manufactured MK 2 gas munitions were documented. The FDD and ISIS reported on Iran's pharmaceutical-based agent research.

### 6. Accountability and Regulatory Frameworks

Accountability for covert use against civilians is fragmented:

- The Church Committee (1975–1976) exposed COINTELPRO and intelligence abuses, prompting reforms, yet no formal compensation for MKUltra victims followed.
- The OPCW, executive body of the Chemical Weapons Convention, condemned chemical weapons use but lacks independent legal authority to inspect covert sites when a state refuses access; Russia has denied access and threatened to reconsider CWC participation.
- The UN Special Rapporteur on the promotion and protection of freedom of expression called for an immediate moratorium on the sale and transfer of surveillance technology until human-rights safeguards are adopted. The European Parliament's PEGA Committee (2023) found substantial failings in controls over spyware misuse.
- The UN Special Rapporteur on counter-terrorism (Fionnuala Ní Aoláin) warned in 2023 of alarming misuse of high-risk technologies.
- The US placed NSO Group on the Entity List in 2021, restricting its access to US technology.

Caveats and conflicts: Havana syndrome remains disputed; the 2022 CIA assessment ruled out a sustained hostile campaign in 976 of 1,000 cases, while a 2024 CBS/60 Minutes investigation reported links to Russian intelligence — the two positions conflict. Russia and Iran deny chemical-weapons use; their denials conflict with OPCW and independent laboratory findings. NSO Group denies systematic misuse of Pegasus, which conflicts with forensic findings by Amnesty International and the Pegasus Project consortium. Claims of satellite-based mind-control harassment of individuals (surveillanceissues.com) are not corroborated by primary or mainstream sources and are excluded from verified findings.

## Open Questions

- Whether Havana syndrome is attributable to directed-energy weapons, environmental factors, or pre-existing conditions remains unresolved across US intelligence assessments and investigative journalism.
- The full extent of covert experimentation programs is unknowable where records were destroyed (MKUltra) or remain classified.
- The scope of Pegasus deployment by client governments — and the full victim list — is not publicly complete; the leaked 50,000-number list is a selection list, not a confirmed-infection list.
- The OPCW's lack of independent inspection authority over undeclared covert sites limits verification of state chemical-weapons programmes.
- The current scale of law-enforcement Stingray use is poorly documented because agencies often obtain devices under non-disclosure agreements.

## Recommendations / Next Steps

1. Monitor implementation of the UN Special Rapporteur's recommended moratorium on commercial spyware sales and the EU AI Act's implications for surveillance technology.
2. Examine national legislation governing law-enforcement use of cell-site simulators and directed-energy devices, focusing on warrant requirements and proportionality.
3. Track OPCW reform proposals that would grant independent inspection authority over suspected covert chemical-weapons sites.
4. For researchers: prioritise primary, declassified, and court-record sources; treat uncorroborated individual claims of advanced technological harassment with caution absent forensic corroboration.
5. For civil-society organisations: document and preserve forensic evidence (device imaging, chemical-residue samples) promptly, given evidentiary decay and the difficulty of attributing covert actions.
