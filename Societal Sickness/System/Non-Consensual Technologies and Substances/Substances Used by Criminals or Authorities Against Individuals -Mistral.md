# Substances Used by Criminals or Authorities Against Individuals

## Question

This report investigates the range of chemical and biological substances that are used either by criminal actors against individuals or by state authorities (law enforcement, military) against individuals, including their classifications, effects, legal status, and notable case studies.

## Executive Summary

1. **Chemical Warfare Agents** represent the most lethal category, with nerve agents (Sarin, VX, Novichok), blister agents (Mustard gas, Lewisite), blood agents (Hydrogen Cyanide), and choking agents (Chlorine, Phosgene) designed for mass casualty scenarios. The Chemical Weapons Convention prohibits their development, production, stockpiling, and use.

2. **Riot Control Agents** (RCAs) such as CS gas, CN gas (Mace), and OC pepper spray are explicitly permitted for law enforcement under the CWC, though banned in warfare. These are non-lethal harassing agents causing temporary incapacitation through sensory irritation.

3. **Incapacitating Agents** including BZ, LSD, and tranquilizers like KOLOKOL-1 produce psychological or physiological effects lasting over 24 hours with low probability of permanent injury, though fatalities up to 5 percent may occur.

4. **Criminal Substances** prominently feature date rape drugs (GHB, Rohypnol, Ketamine) characterized by rapid onset, amnesic effects, and short detection windows (6-12 hours for GHB, up to 60 hours for Rohypnol), as well as poisons like Arsenic, Polonium-210, and Ricin used in targeted assassinations.

5. **Legal Frameworks** are anchored by the Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC) administered by the OPCW, which maintains Schedule 1 (highly toxic with limited legitimate use), Schedule 2 (toxic chemicals with some legitimate use), and Schedule 3 (dual-use chemicals) lists, with Novichok agents added to Schedule 1 in 2020.

6. **Notable Case Studies** include the 2018 Novichok poisoning of Sergei Skripal in Salisbury, UK; the 2006 Polonium-210 poisoning of Alexander Litvinenko in London; and historical incidents like the 1900 English beer poisoning with arsenic affecting over 6,000 people.

7. **Detection and Response** capabilities have advanced through systems like EPA's PHILIS mobile laboratories and ASPECT airborne detection, supporting rapid on-site analysis and decision-making for chemical agent incidents.

## Methodology

### Search Angles

The investigation employed a multi-vector search strategy covering:

- **Classification Systems**: Official categorizations from OPCW, EPA, and medical authorities on chemical warfare agents and riot control substances
- **Criminal Modus Operandi**: Forensic and law enforcement sources on substances used in drug-facilitated crimes and poisonings
- **Legal and Regulatory Frameworks**: International treaties (CWC) and national implementations regarding prohibited and permitted substances
- **Case Studies**: Documented incidents of both criminal and state use to illustrate real-world application
- **Technical Specifications**: Chemical properties, mechanisms of action, detection windows, and treatment protocols

### Source Types Prioritized

1. Government and intergovernmental organizations (EPA, OPCW, WHO, CDC)
2. Peer-reviewed medical and scientific literature (NCBI, ScienceDirect)
3. Authoritative reference works (Wikipedia for comprehensive listings)
4. Forensic and law enforcement publications
5. News and case study documentation for recent incidents

### Limitations

- Classification systems vary slightly between sources regarding agent categories
- Detection windows for some substances (particularly GHB) are extremely limited, affecting forensic investigations
- Some advanced agents (Novichok, fourth-generation agents) have limited publicly available technical data
- Legal status varies by jurisdiction for certain substances

## Findings

### 1. Chemical Warfare Agents Used Primarily by Authorities/Military

#### 1.1 Nerve Agents

Nerve agents disrupt chemical communication in the nervous system by blocking acetylcholinesterase, causing accumulation of acetylcholine and resulting in perpetual nerve excitation, muscle contraction, and eventual respiratory failure.

**G-Series Agents** (High volatility, nonpersistent to semipersistent):
- Tabun (GA): Developed in Germany in 1936, first nerve agent discovered
- Sarin (GB): Approximately 20 times more toxic than cyanide
- Soman (GD): More persistent than Sarin
- Cyclosarin (GF): Similar properties to Sarin

**V-Series Agents** (Low volatility, persistent, liquid contact hazard):
- VE, VG, VM, VX: VX is considered the most toxic, with persistence allowing contamination to remain hazardous for extended periods

**GV-Series and Fourth Generation Agents**:
- Novichok agents: Developed in the former Soviet Union (1970s-1990s), designed to be highly toxic, untraceable, and undetectable. Low volatility nerve agents that evaporate less readily than VX. At least as potent as VX. Added to CWC Schedule 1 in 2020 following the Skripal poisoning incident.

**Mechanism**: Inhibition of acetylcholinesterase enzyme, leading to hyper-stimulation of muscles and respiratory failure.

#### 1.2 Blister Agents (Vesicants)

Blister agents cause severe damage to skin, eyes, respiratory tract, and other tissues through chemical burns and inflammation.

**Sulfur Mustards**:
- Bis(2-chloroethyl) sulfide (Mustard gas; HD): Most well-known, causes large fluid-filled blisters
- Sesquimustard (Q), HN1, HN2, HN3: Nitrogen mustards with similar effects

**Arsenicals**:
- Lewisite (L): 2-Chlorovinyldichloroarsine, causes immediate pain and tissue damage
- Ethyldichloroarsine (ED), Methyldichloroarsine (MD), Phenyldichloroarsine (PD)

**Other Blister Agents**:
- Phosgene oxime (CX): Urticant causing painful wheals on skin

**Effects**: Skin irritation progressing to blister formation, eye damage, respiratory tract injury, bone marrow suppression.

#### 1.3 Blood Agents

Blood agents inhibit cellular oxygen utilization, causing metabolic disruption at the cellular level.

- Hydrogen Cyanide (AC): Interferes with cytochrome oxidase, preventing oxygen utilization
- Cyanogen Chloride (CK): Similar mechanism to hydrogen cyanide
- Arsine (SA): Affects red blood cells, causing hemolysis

**Effects**: Rapid onset of symptoms including headache, dizziness, weakness, confusion, seizures, respiratory failure, and death within minutes to hours depending on dose.

#### 1.4 Choking Agents (Pulmonary Agents)

Choking agents cause injury to the lung-blood barrier, resulting in asphyxia through fluid accumulation in the lungs.

- Chlorine (CL): Greenish-yellow gas with pungent odor, used extensively in World War I
- Phosgene (CG): Colorless gas with odor of moldy hay, delayed onset of symptoms
- Chloropicrin (PS): Greenish-yellow liquid with pungent odor
- Diphosgene (DP): Colorless liquid with suffocating odor

**Effects**: Coughing, choking, chest tightness, pulmonary edema, respiratory failure.

### 2. Riot Control Agents Used by Authorities

Riot Control Agents (RCAs), also known as harassing agents or lacrimators, are substances not intended to kill or injure but rather to temporarily incapacitate through sensory irritation.

#### 2.1 Primary Riot Control Agents

- **CS Gas** (o-chlorobenzylidenemalononitrile): Developed in 1928, first used by British army in 1958. More effective in open air than CN. Primarily used by law enforcement. Causes severe facial pain, reflex blepharospasm, and lacrimation through TRPA1 receptor activation.

- **CN Gas** (Chloroacetophenone, Mace): Developed at end of World War I. Less potent than CS but historically significant. Now largely replaced by CS for law enforcement use.

- **OC** (Oleoresin Capsicum, Pepper Spray): Active agent is capsaicin, extracted from Capsicum plants. Developed in late 1970s, adopted by law enforcement in early 1980s. Acts on TRPV1 receptors. Popular for civilian use.

- **CR Gas** (Dibenzoxazepine): Most potent TRPA1 agonist among traditional tear gases.

#### 2.2 Other Harassing Agents

- **Vomiting Agents** (Sternators): Adamsite (DM), Diphenylchloroarsine (DA), Diphenylcyanoarsine (DC). Cause congestion, coughing, sneezing, nausea.

- **Malodorants**: Compounds with strong unpleasant smells producing aversive effects without toxic effects.

**Deployment Methods**: Aerosol dispersion via grenades, canisters, handheld spray devices, water cannons, or large-scale dispersion systems.

**Legal Status**: Banned for use in warfare since 1995, but explicitly permitted for law enforcement and civilian self-defense under the Chemical Weapons Convention.

**Epidemiology**: In the United States, NPDS data from 2017 reported 4,007 exposures to lacrimators: 83 percent OC, 12 percent CN, 0.2 percent CS, 4 percent other/unknown. Twenty-five percent required healthcare facility evaluation, with most having minor effects and no deaths reported.

### 3. Incapacitating Agents

Incapacitating agents produce debilitating effects with limited probability of permanent injury or loss of life. Effects typically last over 24 hours, and medical evacuation is recommended though not required for complete recovery.

- **Psychological Agents**: 3-Quinuclidinyl benzilate (BZ), Phencyclidine (PCP, SN), Lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD). Cause mental disturbances including delirium and hallucinations.

- **Other Incapacitating Agents**: KOLOKOL-1 (tranquilizer). Operates through interactions outside the central nervous system.

**Fatality Rate**: Up to 5 percent with use of these agents.

### 4. Substances Used by Criminals Against Individuals

#### 4.1 Drug-Facilitated Sexual Assault (Date Rape Drugs)

These substances are characterized by their ability to be covertly administered, rapid onset of effects, amnesic properties, and short detection windows.

- **GHB** (Gamma-Hydroxybutyrate/Gamma-Hydroxybutyric Acid):
  - **Effects**: Sedation, euphoria, amnesia, loss of consciousness. Onset in 15-30 minutes, duration 3-4 hours.
  - **Detection Window**: 6-12 hours in urine (some sources indicate 8-10 hours). Extremely challenging for forensic investigation due to short window.
  - **Characteristics**: Odorless, colorless, tasteless (though may have slight salty taste). Can be produced as liquid, powder, or capsule.
  - **Risk**: A GHB-spiked drink can cause victim to pass out for 2-3 hours. Combined with alcohol, can cause respiratory failure and death.

- **Rohypnol** (Flunitrazepam):
  - **Effects**: Sedation, muscle relaxation, amnesia. Effects occur within 30 minutes, lasting several hours.
  - **Detection Window**: Up to 60 hours in urine (rarely detected beyond 24 hours with current technology).
  - **Characteristics**: Benzodiazepine, sometimes known as "roofies." Relatively inexpensive. Not produced or prescribed in the United States; importation banned.
  - **Presentation**: White tablet, can be crushed and dissolved in drinks.

- **Ketamine**:
  - **Effects**: Dissociative anesthetic, causes hallucinations, distorted perceptions, amnesia. Fast-acting.
  - **Detection Window**: Variable, can be detected in urine.
  - **Characteristics**: Veterinary anesthesia. Available as liquid, powder, or capsule. Also a street drug.
  - **Presentation**: Can be added to drinks or administered directly.

- **Other Substances**: Alcohol and MDMA (ecstasy) are also commonly used in drug-facilitated sexual assaults.

**Forensic Challenges**: Delay in specimen collection can profoundly impact prosecutorial and toxicological outcomes. Urine is generally a better specimen than blood for detection of most date rape drugs.

#### 4.2 Poisons Used in Criminal Homicides

- **Arsenic**:
  - **Types**: Inorganic arsenic compounds (arsenite, arsenate), organic arsenic compounds.
  - **Acute Poisoning Symptoms**: Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea (may contain blood), leading to severe dehydration and hypovolemic shock. Neurological effects include seizures, coma, cognitive impairment.
  - **Chronic Exposure Effects**: Hyperpigmentation/hypopigmentation, thickening of skin on palms and soles, Mees' lines on fingernails, peripheral neuropathy, cardiovascular effects, increased cancer risk (lung, liver, bladder, prostate, blood vessels, skin).
  - **Mechanism**: Inhibits cellular respiration, disrupts ATP production.
  - **Notable Cases**: 1900 English beer poisoning affected over 6,000 people with more than 70 deaths due to arsenic-contaminated sugar in brewing process.

- **Polonium-210**:
  - **Type**: Radioactive isotope
  - **Effects**: Radiation poisoning causing organ failure. Symptoms include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fatigue, hair loss.
  - **Notable Case**: Alexander Litvinenko poisoning in London, 2006. Administered via tea, resulted in death within weeks. High-profile case demonstrating use as assassination tool.

- **Ricin**:
  - **Source**: Natural toxin from castor beans (Ricinus communis)
  - **Mechanism**: Inhibits protein synthesis by inactivating ribosomes
  - **Effects**: Depending on route of exposure (inhalation, ingestion, injection): fever, cough, pulmonary edema (inhalation); nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, seizures, organ failure (ingestion)
  - **Classification**: Listed as a chemical weapon by the Chemical Weapons Convention

- **Cyanide**:
  - **Forms**: Hydrogen cyanide gas, cyanide salts (potassium cyanide, sodium cyanide)
  - **Mechanism**: Inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, preventing cellular oxygen utilization
  - **Effects**: Rapid onset (seconds to minutes): headache, dizziness, weakness, confusion, seizures, respiratory failure, death
  - **Notable Cases**: Used in various criminal poisonings and suicides

### 5. Legal and Regulatory Frameworks

#### 5.1 Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC)

- **Administered by**: Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons (OPCW)
- **Definition of Chemical Weapon**: "A chemical used to cause intentional death or harm through its toxic properties. Munitions, devices and other equipment specifically designed to weaponize toxic chemicals also fall under the definition of chemical weapons. This definition includes toxic chemicals and their precursors."
- **Prohibition**: Development, production, stockpiling, and use of chemical weapons
- **Permitted Uses**: Riot control agents explicitly permitted for law enforcement purposes

#### 5.2 CWC Schedules of Chemicals

**Schedule 1**: Chemicals with high toxicity that have limited or no legitimate uses. Strict control measures. Includes:
- Nerve agents: Tabun, Sarin, Soman, VX
- Blister agents: Sulfur Mustard, Lewisite
- Blood agents: Hydrogen Cyanide, etc.
- **2020 Addition**: Novichok nerve agent family added to Schedule 1 following UK assassination attempts

**Schedule 2**: Toxic chemicals with some legitimate small-scale applications. Includes:
- Certain precursors to Schedule 1 chemicals
- Some riot control agent precursors

**Schedule 3**: Dual-use chemicals with legitimate large-scale industrial applications. Includes:
- Chemicals that can be used as precursors to chemical weapons but have significant commercial uses

#### 5.3 National Regulations

- **United States**: EPA's Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear Consequence Management Advisory Team (CBRN CMAT) provides subject matter expertise. Chemical warfare agents are regulated under various environmental and public health laws.
- **United Kingdom**: Chemical Weapons Act and other legislation implement CWC obligations. The 2018 Novichok poisoning in Salisbury led to enhanced chemical defense capabilities.

### 6. Detection, Response, and Medical Countermeasures

#### 6.1 Detection Systems

- **EPA PHILIS** (Portable High-throughput Integrated Laboratory Identification System): Mobile laboratory for on-site analysis of environmental samples contaminated with chemical warfare agents and toxic industrial compounds. Reduces turn-around time for data, enhances on-site decisions, provides reliable data for facility clearance.

- **EPA ASPECT** (Airborne Spectral Photometric Environmental Collection Technology): Airborne real-time chemical and radiological detection platform. Can deploy within one hour, supports hazardous substance response.

- **Environmental Response Laboratory Network (ERLN)**: Comprehensive laboratory network providing analytical data for environmental emergency response.

#### 6.2 Medical Countermeasures

**Nerve Agent Exposure**:
- Atropine: Anticholinergic that blocks muscarinic receptors
- Pralidoxime chloride (2-PAM): Reactivates acetylcholinesterase
- Diazepam: Anticonvulsant
- Decontamination: Removal of clothing, washing with soap and water

**Cyanide Exposure**:
- Sodium nitrite/sodium thiosulfate: Converts hemoglobin to methemoglobin which binds cyanide
- Hydroxocobalamin: Binds cyanide to form cyanocobalamin
- Supportive care: Oxygen therapy, seizure control

**Riot Control Agent Exposure**:
- Decontamination: Removal from contaminated area, removal of clothing, flushing with water or saline
- Symptomatic treatment: Pain management, respiratory support
- Most exposures result in minor effects requiring minimal medical intervention

**Date Rape Drug Exposure**:
- Supportive care: Monitoring, respiratory support if needed
- Forensic evidence collection: Urine sample within detection window (6-12 hours for GHB, up to 60 hours for Rohypnol)
- Psychological support: Counseling for trauma

### 7. Notable Case Studies

#### 7.1 Novichok Poisoning - Salisbury, UK (2018)

- **Target**: Sergei Skripal (former Russian military intelligence officer) and his daughter Yulia
- **Agent**: Novichok nerve agent (fourth-generation agent)
- **Method**: Applied to door handle of Skripal's residence
- **Impact**: Both victims survived after prolonged hospitalization. One bystander (Dawn Sturgess) died after handling a discarded perfume bottle containing the agent. A police officer was also hospitalized.
- **Significance**: First known use of Novichok agents outside Russia. Led to OPCW adding Novichok family to CWC Schedule 1 in 2020. Demonstrated persistence of these agents in the environment.

#### 7.2 Polonium-210 Poisoning - London, UK (2006)

- **Target**: Alexander Litvinenko (former FSB officer)
- **Agent**: Polonium-210 (radioactive isotope)
- **Method**: Administered in tea at a London hotel
- **Impact**: Litvinenko died 22 days after exposure. Radiation contamination was detected at multiple locations in London.
- **Significance**: High-profile assassination demonstrating use of radioactive materials as poisons. Led to enhanced radiation detection capabilities in the UK.

#### 7.3 Arsenic Beer Poisoning - England (1900)

- **Scale**: Over 6,000 people poisoned, more than 70 deaths
- **Source**: Arsenic-contaminated sugar used in brewing
- **Impact**: Widespread illness across Midlands and North West England, with Manchester most heavily affected
- **Significance**: Demonstrated potential for mass poisoning through contaminated food supply. Investigation revealed arsenic had been unknowingly poisoning beer drinkers for decades prior.

#### 7.4 Sarin Attacks - Japan (1994-1995)

- **Perpetrator**: Aum Shinrikyo cult
- **Agent**: Sarin nerve agent
- **Methods**: Aerosol release in Matsumoto city (1994) and Tokyo subway (1995)
- **Impact**: Matsumoto attack: 8 deaths, 500+ injured. Tokyo subway attack: 13 deaths, 6,000+ injured.
- **Significance**: First major use of chemical weapons by a non-state actor in modern times. Demonstrated vulnerability of civilian populations to chemical attacks.

### Conflicts and Caveats

- **Detection Windows**: Sources vary on GHB detection window (6-12 hours vs 8-10 hours). The shorter window is more conservative and reflects forensic best practices.
- **Novichok Classification**: Some sources classify Novichok as GV-series, others as fourth-generation agents. The OPCW's 2020 addition to Schedule 1 provides official classification.
- **Riot Control Agent Statistics**: US data from 2017 may not reflect current usage patterns. However, the dominance of OC (pepper spray) over CN and CS is consistent across time periods.
- **Fatality Rates**: Incapacitating agent fatality rate of up to 5 percent is an estimate and may vary based on conditions and specific agents used.

## Open Questions

1. **Emerging Threats**: What new chemical agents are being developed that could be used by criminals or authorities? Monitoring of scientific literature and intelligence sources is needed.

2. **Detection Gaps**: How can detection windows for substances like GHB be extended to improve forensic investigations? Research into more sensitive analytical methods is ongoing.

3. **Legal Harmonization**: How can international legal frameworks be strengthened to address gaps in regulating dual-use chemicals that could be weaponized?

4. **Attribution Challenges**: How can the source of chemical attacks be more reliably attributed, particularly for state-sponsored incidents? Advances in forensic chemistry and intelligence sharing are critical.

5. **Public Health Preparedness**: What additional measures are needed to prepare healthcare systems for mass casualty chemical incidents? Training, equipment stockpiling, and coordination protocols require continuous evaluation.

## Recommendations and Next Steps

### For Authorities and Policy Makers

1. **Enhance Detection Capabilities**: Invest in portable detection systems like PHILIS and airborne platforms like ASPECT to enable rapid response to chemical incidents.

2. **Strengthen Legal Frameworks**: Continue to update CWC schedules to include newly identified chemical threats, as demonstrated by the 2020 Novichok addition.

3. **Improve Forensic Capabilities**: Develop more sensitive testing methods for substances with short detection windows, particularly GHB and other date rape drugs.

4. **Public Health Preparedness**: Ensure healthcare providers are trained in recognizing and treating chemical exposure, with appropriate stockpiles of antidotes and decontamination equipment.

5. **International Cooperation**: Strengthen information sharing and joint response capabilities between nations to address transnational chemical threats.

### For Law Enforcement

1. **Training**: Provide comprehensive training on recognizing signs of drug-facilitated crimes and proper evidence collection procedures.

2. **Public Awareness**: Educate the public on the risks of date rape drugs and how to protect against covert administration.

3. **Rapid Testing**: Implement rapid testing capabilities for common date rape drugs at medical facilities and law enforcement agencies.

### For Research Community

1. **Agent Characterization**: Continue research into the properties, detection, and medical countermeasures for emerging chemical threats.

2. **Forensic Methodology**: Develop improved analytical methods for detecting trace amounts of substances in biological samples and environmental matrices.

3. **Medical Countermeasures**: Investigate new treatments and antidotes for chemical exposures, particularly for nerve agents and novel toxins.

### For General Public

1. **Awareness**: Be informed about the existence and characteristics of substances that could be used maliciously.

2. **Safety Measures**: Take precautions such as not accepting drinks from strangers, watching drinks being prepared, and seeking immediate medical attention if sudden unexplained symptoms occur.

3. **Reporting**: Report any suspected incidents to authorities promptly to enable timely investigation and evidence collection.
