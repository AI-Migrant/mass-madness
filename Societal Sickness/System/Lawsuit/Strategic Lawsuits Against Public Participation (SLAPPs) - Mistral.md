# Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation (SLAPPs)

## Question

What are Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation (SLAPPs), how do they function, what harm do they cause, and how have jurisdictions responded to them?

## Executive Summary

1. SLAPPs are lawsuits or threats of legal action brought primarily to censor, intimidate, and silence critics — not to obtain a favourable judgment. The plaintiff's objective is met if the defendant abandons the criticism out of fear, mounting legal costs, or exhaustion.
2. The term was coined in the 1980s by sociologists Penelope Canan and George Pring and popularised in their 1996 book *SLAPPs: Getting Sued for Speaking Out*.
3. SLAPPs operate through resource asymmetry: a better-resourced party weaponises the cost of litigation against a journalist, activist, NGO, academic, or ordinary citizen. A 2022 Reporters Committee survey found that 59% of journalists sued for defamation altered their coverage as a result — not because their reporting was wrong, but because of the cost and stress of defending it.
4. There is no federal anti-SLAPP statute in the United States. As of March 2026, 40 states and the District of Columbia have enacted anti-SLAPP laws of varying strength, leaving roughly 14% of the population without protection.
5. The European Union adopted Directive (EU) 2024/1069 ("Daphne's law") on 11 April 2024, providing early dismissal, cost protection, and penalties for abusive cross-border civil claims. The transposition deadline was 7 May 2026.
6. The United Kingdom has moved incrementally: the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 introduced limited measures for SLAPPs linked to economic crime, while the Government backed a broader Private Members' Bill (Strategic Litigation Against Public Participation Bill) that passed Second Reading on 23 February 2024.
7. The core policy tension is balance: anti-SLAPP mechanisms that are too broad risk blocking meritorious defamation and privacy claims, while mechanisms that are too narrow fail to deter abusive litigation.

## Methodology

The investigation drew on four search angles: (1) definitional and academic sources on the nature and history of SLAPPs; (2) primary legal instruments — the EU Directive, the Council of Europe Recommendation, UK government publications, and US state-law compendia; (3) documented case examples and empirical evidence of chilling effects; and (4) jurisdictional reform debates and criticisms. Sources include Wikipedia, the European Commission, EUR-Lex, the European Parliament, the Council of Europe, GOV.UK, the UK Commons Library, the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, the Institute for Free Speech, the OHCHR, and legal-practice commentary.

Limitations: the precise number of US anti-SLAPP jurisdictions is a moving target and sources disagree at different dates (28 in one compendium, 32 in April 2022, 33 in late 2023, 40 in March 2026). This report uses the most recent figure with its date. Empirical data on SLAPP frequency is incomplete because plaintiffs rarely label their suits as such and many jurisdictions do not track SLAPP claims.

## Findings

### 1. Definition and Origin

Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation are lawsuits or threats of legal action that use abusive litigation tactics with the aim or effect of suppressing public participation and critical reporting on matters of public interest. In a typical SLAPP, the plaintiff does not expect to win; the goals are accomplished if the defendant succumbs to fear, intimidation, mounting legal costs, or exhaustion and abandons the criticism. A SLAPP may also intimidate others from joining the debate — the secondary chilling effect. SLAPPs are often preceded by a legal threat and are difficult to penalise because plaintiffs attempt to obfuscate their intent to censor.

The term was coined by sociologists Penelope Canan and George Pring in the 1980s and formalised in their 1996 book *SLAPPs: Getting Sued for Speaking Out*. Originally the concept centred on suits targeting non-governmental speakers who petitioned government, but it has since broadened to cover litigation aimed at anyone contributing to public debate.

### 2. How SLAPPs Function

SLAPPs are characterised by several recurring features:

- Resource asymmetry: a wealthy corporation or individual sues a less-resourced critic, so the cost of defence is itself the punishment.
- Manifestly unfounded or exaggerated claims, including exorbitant damages demands designed to overwhelm.
- Collateral purpose: the suit's true aim is not redress but silence.
- Procedural drag: even a meritless suit can take years and many thousands of pounds or dollars to defend.
- Beyond civil defamation: SLAPPs also use criminal defamation, privacy and data protection claims, and administrative actions. In some jurisdictions (Italy, Serbia, India) criminal defamation is a common SLAPP vector.
- Secondary chilling: the visible punishment of one critic deters others.

### 3. Notable Cases

- Murray Energy v. HBO (2017): West Virginia coal executive Robert Murray sued HBO and John Oliver for defamation after a *Last Week Tonight* segment criticising his company's safety record. The case was dismissed in under a year and the appeal failed. It became a textbook example of a resource-asymmetric SLAPP.
- Putin's People / Catherine Belton (2021): Several Russian oligarchs, including Roman Abramovich, brought a libel action against HarperCollins and journalist Catherine Belton over her book *Putin's People*. Despite prevailing, Belton faced approximately £1.5 million in legal costs. The case was described by former cabinet minister David Davis as a SLAPP.
- Dole Fruit v. Fredrik Gertten (2010): Filmmaker Gertten won an anti-SLAPP motion after Dole sued him for defamation over a documentary about farm workers.
- MagicJack v. Boing Boing (2009): Dismissed as a SLAPP by a California judge, with MagicJack ordered to pay most of Boing Boing's legal costs.
- Daphne Caruana Galizia: The Maltese investigative journalist, murdered by a car bomb in 2017, had multiple libel suits pending against her at the time of her death. Her case catalysed European legislative action and the EU Directive is informally called "Daphne's law."
- Local-level SLAPPs: The most common cases receive little national attention — a city council member suing a constituent who testified at a public meeting, a school board member suing a parent for a Facebook post, a sheriff threatening defamation against a reporter who filed records requests.

### 4. The Chilling Effect

A 2022 survey by the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press found that 59% of journalists sued for defamation reported altering their coverage as a result — not because they concluded their reporting was wrong, but because of the cost and stress of the litigation. SLAPPs can also raise the cost of directors' and officers' liability insurance for media and civil-society organisations, interfering with their ability to operate. The OHCHR commissioned a study from the University of Aberdeen concluding that SLAPPs adversely affect freedoms of expression, assembly, association, and the right to public participation.

### 5. Jurisdictional Responses

#### United States

There is no federal anti-SLAPP statute. The SPEAK FREE Act of 2015, a bipartisan federal bill, stalled in the House Judiciary Subcommittee and expired at the end of the 114th Congress. Courts are divided on whether state anti-SLAPP statutes apply to claims brought in federal court.

At state level, protection has expanded steadily. As of March 2026, 40 states and the District of Columbia have anti-SLAPP laws. The Institute for Free Speech reports that 86.2% of the population lives in a state with an anti-SLAPP law, and 62.9% under laws rated "good." New Jersey became the 33rd state in September 2023 (based on the Uniform Public Expression Act). States without statutes include some large jurisdictions; Colorado and West Virginia have recognised anti-SLAPP-like protection through case law.

Typical features of US state anti-SLAPP statutes include: a motion to strike claims arising from protected speech; an early dismissal before costly discovery; an automatic stay of discovery once the motion is filed; an award of attorney's fees and costs to successful defendants; and an immediate right of appeal from denial. The strongest laws are ranked in California, Nevada, Tennessee, Vermont, Georgia, and Oklahoma.

#### European Union

The EU Anti-SLAPP Directive — formally Directive (EU) 2024/1069 of 11 April 2024 on protecting persons who engage in public participation from manifestly unfounded claims or abusive court proceedings — was published in the Official Journal on 16 April 2024, entered into force on 6 May 2024, and is informally known as "Daphne's law." Key features:

- Scope: civil and commercial matters with cross-border implications; applies to natural or legal persons engaging in public participation, especially journalists and human rights defenders.
- Early dismissal: judges may identify SLAPPs and order their early dismissal, sparing defendants from defending manifestly unfounded claims brought in bad faith.
- Cost protection: reimbursement of legal costs incurred by SLAPP victims, unless shown to be excessive.
- Deterrence: judges may impose penalties or other measures on those initiating SLAPPs.
- Support: Member States must facilitate the involvement of associations, organisations, and trade unions in assisting defendants.
- Transposition deadline: 7 May 2026.
- Complementary instrument: an EU Recommendation adopted 27 April 2022, applicable immediately.

A recognised limitation is the cross-border requirement: the Directive does not cover purely domestic SLAPPs, and several Member States (notably Germany) are considering broader national measures beyond the Directive's minimum. The Directive also does not prevent abusive multi-state litigation based on online personality or copyright infringements; reform of the Brussels Ibis and Rome II regulations is proposed to address this.

#### Council of Europe

On 5 April 2024 the Committee of Ministers adopted Recommendation CM/Rec(2024)2 on countering the use of SLAPPs. The Recommendation provides a broad definition of SLAPPs and applies to civil, administrative, and criminal contexts, and to both domestic and cross-border cases (including multiple, coordinated, and anonymous-participation SLAPPs). It complements the narrower EU Directive by extending beyond cross-border civil matters.

#### United Kingdom

The UK response has been incremental:

- Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 (ECCTA): introduced a new early dismissal mechanism and costs protection for SLAPPs linked to economic crime, where a defendant can show the case falls within a statutory definition of SLAPPs and the claimant cannot prove the claim is likely to succeed. The Russian and Belarusian Legal Services General Licence was amended so it no longer authorises legal fees for defamation and similar cases.
- Government response to call for evidence (July 2022): proposed a quick, cost-limiting early dismissal process using a three-part test — (1) whether the case concerns activity in the public interest, (2) evidence of abuse of process, and (3) whether the case has a realistic prospect of success — plus costs protection for defendants where claims proceed.
- Strategic Litigation Against Public Participation Bill (Wayne David MP): a Private Members' Bill that passed Second Reading on 23 February 2024 with Government backing. It would extend protection beyond economic crime to all litigation types, including sexual harassment, and create a new dismissal mechanism. Following amendments, the original subjective intent test in Clause 2(1) was replaced with an objective test.
- UK Anti-SLAPP Coalition: published a Model Anti-SLAPP Law in November 2022 in consultation with senior media lawyers, recommending a "likelihood of prevailing" threshold and ten specific hallmarks of abuse, designed to fit within the Ministry of Justice framework.
- Context: provisional data from the Coalition Against SLAPPs in Europe estimated 14 SLAPP cases in England and Wales in 2021, up from 2 in 2019 and 2020. The Defamation Act 2013 introduced a "serious harm" threshold and curbed libel tourism.

#### Other Jurisdictions

- Punjab Defamation Act 2024 (Pakistan): criticised by journalists and human rights groups as enabling SLAPP-type suits, with fines up to Rs 30 lakh and special tribunals; challenged in the Lahore High Court.
- Italy: criminal defamation frequently used against journalists reporting on public figures and entrepreneurs (e.g. Amalia De Simone).
- Serbia: SLAPPs were rare after the overthrow of Milošević but reappeared in the late 2010s and early 2020s.
- Thailand: journalists such as Chutima Sidasathian face defamation charges for public-interest reporting.

### 6. Criticisms and Tensions

- Over-inclusion: anti-SLAPP statutes that are too broad may block meritorious libel, privacy, and data-protection claims, denying access to justice for genuine reputational harm.
- Identification difficulty: because plaintiffs obfuscate their censorial intent, courts may be reluctant to infer an improper purpose, weakening subjective-intent tests; the UK moved from a subjective to an objective test for this reason.
- Federal gap (US): the absence of a federal statute means protection depends on state law, and courts are split on whether state anti-SLAPP statutes apply in federal court.
- Cross-border limitation (EU): the Directive's cross-border requirement leaves purely domestic SLAPPs unaddressed at EU level, creating implementation divergence between Member States.
- Balance of rights: claimant-side respondents in the UK consultation stressed that the existing balance between reputation, privacy, and expression in defamation law should not be upset, and that an "actual malice" threshold was the strongest area of concern.

## Open Questions

- Whether and when the UK Strategic Litigation Against Public Participation Bill will complete its passage; at the time of research it had passed Second Reading but had not completed Committee and remaining stages.
- How many EU Member States transposed Directive (EU) 2024/1069 by the 7 May 2026 deadline, and whether national implementations extend beyond cross-border cases.
- Whether the United States will enact a federal anti-SLAPP statute or resolve the circuit split on application of state statutes in federal court.
- The long-term deterrent effect of anti-SLAPP penalties versus the risk of deterring meritorious defamation claims — empirical evidence remains thin.
- The scope and enforcement of the Punjab Defamation Act 2024 and whether it functions as an enabling instrument for SLAPPs.

## Recommendations / Next Steps

- For anyone facing a legal threat that may be a SLAPP: seek immediate specialist legal advice; do not assume the merits alone will protect you from cost and delay; check whether your jurisdiction has an early-dismissment mechanism.
- For UK-based defendants: monitor the progress of the Strategic Litigation Against Public Participation Bill and the ECCTA secondary legislation; the early-dismissment mechanism currently covers only economic-crime-linked SLAPPs.
- For policy actors: consider whether domestic (non-cross-border) SLAPPs warrant separate national measures, as the EU Directive leaves that gap.
- For further research: compare implementation of Directive (EU) 2024/1069 across Member States; track the USLAPP federal question; and gather systematic empirical data on SLAPP frequency and chilling effects, which current estimates only approximate.
