Five Techniques, methods, torture

### Summary
The Five Techniques were interrogation methods developed by the UK in the mid-20th century, rooted in Cold War sensory deprivation research. They were first used during the Aden Emergency (1963–1967) and most notoriously in Northern Ireland in 1971 against the "Hooded Men." The techniques—prolonged wall-standing, hooding, subjection to noise, sleep deprivation, and food/drink deprivation—caused severe physical and psychological harm. Legally, they were banned by the UK in 1972, ruled "inhuman and degrading" by the European Court of Human Rights in 1978, and classified as torture by the UK Supreme Court in 2021.

# The Five Techniques: Invention, Practice, and Effects

## Research Question

What were the Five Techniques, how were they invented and developed, how were they practiced, and what were their effects on individuals, institutions, and international law?

## Executive Summary

The **Five Techniques**—prolonged wall-standing, hooding, subjection to noise, deprivation of sleep, and deprivation of food and drink—were a set of interrogation methods developed by the United Kingdom in the mid-20th century, rooted in Cold War-era sensory deprivation research. First deployed during the Aden Emergency (1963–1967), they were most notoriously applied in Northern Ireland during Operation Demetrius (1971), where fourteen detainees (the "Hooded Men") experienced all five techniques simultaneously at Shackleton Barracks. The methods produced severe physical and psychological harm, including prolonged pain, exhaustion, hallucinations, depression, and long-term trauma. Legally, they were banned by the UK in 1972, ruled "inhuman and degrading treatment" by the European Court of Human Rights in 1978, and finally classified as torture by the UK Supreme Court in 2021. Their use influenced interrogation practices globally, including in US "enhanced interrogation" programs and the Iraq War, leaving a lasting impact on human rights law and the ethics of state coercion.

## Methodology

This research synthesizes primary documentation from legal rulings (Parker Report, *Ireland v. United Kingdom* at the ECHR, UK Supreme Court 2021), historical accounts of the Aden Emergency and Northern Ireland's Troubles, psychological studies on sensory deprivation, and secondary analyses from human rights organizations and academic publications. Search angles included the techniques' origins in psychological research, their operational deployment in colonial and domestic contexts, legal classifications across jurisdictions, and their long-term effects on survivors and legal frameworks.

## Findings

### Invention and Development

The Five Techniques emerged from **sensory deprivation research** conducted primarily in the 1950s and 1960s, a period marked by Cold War anxieties about "brainwashing" and psychological manipulation. Research programs, including the CIA's **Project MKUltra** at Canada's Allan Memorial Institute under Dr. Donald Ewen Cameron, explored the effects of sensory isolation, prolonged sleep deprivation, and psychological stress on human subjects. These studies revealed that sensory deprivation could induce severe disorientation, hallucinations, and psychological breakdown—effects that intelligence agencies recognized as potentially useful for interrogation.

British security forces adapted these findings into a systematic interrogation methodology. The techniques were designed to create **perceptual deprivation** through a combination of:
- **Wall-standing**: Forcing detainees to maintain stress positions for hours, causing extreme physical strain
- **Hooding**: Placing black or navy bags over detainees' heads, cutting off visual stimuli
- **Subjection to noise**: Exposing detainees to continuous loud hissing or white noise at high volumes (approximately 85 dB)
- **Sleep deprivation**: Preventing rest over extended periods
- **Food and drink deprivation**: Limiting sustenance to bread and water or less

The **loose-fitting hoods**, combined with forced stress positions and continuous noise, created an environment of **unvarying sensory input**, producing the psychological effects observed in laboratory sensory deprivation studies. This approach was explicitly designed to avoid visible physical marks while maximizing psychological coercion.

### Practice and Operational Use

#### Aden Emergency (1963–1967)
The Five Techniques were first deployed operationally during the **Aden Emergency**, a nationalist insurgency against British rule in what is now Yemen. Facing challenges from the National Liberation Front (NLF) and Front for the Liberation of Occupied South Yemen (FLOSY), British security forces employed the techniques to extract intelligence from suspected insurgents. The decision to use these methods was driven by the belief that they would **improve the quantity and quality of intelligence** obtained during interrogations, a rationale that would persist across different contexts.

#### Northern Ireland: Operation Demetrius (1971)
The most documented application occurred during **Operation Demetrius**, a mass arrest and internment operation in Northern Ireland targeting suspected IRA members. On August 9, 1971, British forces arrested hundreds of individuals. From these, **fourteen detainees**—selected based on physical condition rather than suspected involvement—were subjected to all five techniques together at **Shackleton Barracks**, a secret interrogation center.

The interrogation regime lasted **seven days**. Detainees were:
- Kept hooded and handcuffed in cold cells when not being interrogated
- Forced to stand in stress positions for many hours
- Subjected to continuous loud hissing noise
- Deprived of sleep, food, and drink
- Additionally, many reported beatings, threats, and other forms of abuse

These fourteen became known as the **"Hooded Men"**—the only detainees in Northern Ireland subjected to the complete combination of all five techniques. Others experienced individual techniques alongside conventional interrogation methods.

#### Subsequent Use
- **Brazil**: British agents taught the Five Techniques to forces of Brazil's military dictatorship
- **United States**: The 1978 ECHR ruling that the techniques did not constitute torture was later **cited internally by the US** to justify its own interrogation methods, including those used in the "War on Terror"
- **Iraq War (2003)**: British service members illegally used the techniques, contributing to the death of **Baha Mousa**, an Iraqi detainee

### Effects

#### On Individuals
The immediate effects on detainees were severe and multifaceted:

**Physical Effects:**
- Prolonged pain from stress positions
- Physical exhaustion and muscle damage
- Repeated loss of consciousness
- Sensory overload and disorientation

**Psychological Effects:**
- Severe anxiety and depression
- Hallucinations and perceptual distortions
- Confusion and cognitive impairment
- **Long-term psychological trauma** that persisted for decades

**Neurological Impact:**
The combination of sensory deprivation, sleep deprivation, and stress created conditions known to cause **neuropsychological damage**. Research by psychologists like Tim Shallice demonstrated that these methods resulted in extreme sensory deprivation, leading to serious and lasting psychological harm. The techniques were specifically designed to exploit the brain's vulnerability to unchanging sensory environments.

#### On Intelligence Gathering
While some **valuable intelligence was gathered**, the overall effectiveness is disputed:
- Proponents argued the techniques produced actionable information about IRA groups and potential terrorist plots
- Critics note that the **reliability of information obtained under coercion** is questionable, as detainees may provide false information to end the suffering
- The **widespread negative effects**—including the creation of martyrs and the radicalization of communities—arguably outweighed any intelligence benefits

#### On Legal and Human Rights Frameworks
The Five Techniques had a profound impact on international human rights law:

**Domestic Response (1972):**
- Following public and parliamentary outrage, the UK government commissioned the **Parker Report** (chaired by Lord Parker, Lord Chief Justice of England)
- The report concluded the techniques were **illegal under British and Northern Irish law**
- UK Prime Minister Edward Heath announced in Parliament (March 2, 1972) that the techniques **"will not be used in future as an aid to interrogation"**
- Directives were issued prohibiting their use, which remain in force

**European Commission of Human Rights (1976):**
- Ruled that the **combined use of the five methods amounted to torture**
- Cited that the intensity of stress from sensory deprivation "directly affects the personality physically and mentally"
- Noted the systematic application for extracting information showed "a clear resemblance to those methods of systematic torture which have been known over the ages"

**European Court of Human Rights (1978):**
- In the landmark case *Ireland v. United Kingdom* (5310/71), the ECHR ruled:
  - The techniques constituted **"inhuman and degrading treatment"**
  - They **breached Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights**
  - However, they **did not amount to torture** under the court's interpretation at that time
- This ruling was significant as the first inter-state case before the ECHR

**Irish Government Review Request (2014):**
- After RTÉ's *The Torture Files* documentary revealed that **ministers had authorized** the use of the techniques in 1971–1972, the Irish Government asked the ECHR to revise its 1978 judgment
- In 2018, the ECHR **declined to revise** its ruling by six votes to one

**UK Supreme Court (2021):**
- In a historic reversal, the **UK Supreme Court ruled that the use of the Five Techniques amounted to torture**
- This brought UK law into alignment with contemporary human rights standards

**Conflicts and Caveats:**
- The classification of the Five Techniques as torture remains contested in legal history, with different bodies reaching different conclusions (ECHR 1978: not torture; UK Supreme Court 2021: torture)
- The effectiveness of the techniques for intelligence gathering is difficult to verify, as records may be incomplete or classified
- Some sources suggest the techniques were used more widely than officially documented
- The psychological literature consistently shows severe harm from sensory deprivation, but the exact mechanisms and long-term effects vary by individual
