## Examples of CNS‑acting inhalation incapacitating agents
These are the canonical examples used in toxicology and anesthesiology:

### 1. Volatile anesthetics
These are the most controlled and predictable CNS inhalation agents.
- Isoflurane
- Sevoflurane
- Desflurane
- Halothane (older, less used today)

Mechanism: GABA‑A potentiation → CNS depression → unconsciousness  
Used in operating theatres, not in any incapacitating‑agent context.

### 2. Organic solvent vapors
Historically used as anesthetics or sedatives.
- Diethyl ether
- Chloroform
- Nitrous oxide mixed with ether (old anesthetic technique)

Mechanism: lipid‑soluble CNS depression  
These are not pulmonary agents — they do not incapacitate by choking or lung irritation.

### 3. Nitrous oxide
A mild inhalation anesthetic.
- Causes euphoria, dissociation, and at higher doses loss of consciousness
- Often combined with oxygen in medical settings
- CNS mechanism, not pulmonary

## Ranking: How noticeable CNS‑acting inhalation agents are
(From most obvious → least obvious)

### 1. Diethyl ether — extremely noticeable
- Very strong smell (sweet, sharp, instantly recognizable)
- Causes coughing, throat irritation, tearing
- Highly flammable → strong safety odorants
- Detectable at very low concentrations
- Historically used as an anesthetic but impossible to use without people noticing

Verdict: One of the least covert substances in existence.

### 2. Chloroform — very noticeable
- Strong, sweet, heavy odor
- Causes dizziness, irritation, and coughing before sedation
- Requires high concentration for CNS effects
- Slow onset compared to myths
- People smell it immediately unless the dose is dangerously high

Verdict: Noticeable, impractical, medically dangerous.

### 3. Halothane — moderately noticeable
- Distinct but less pungent odor than ether/chloroform
- Still detectable by anyone in the room
- Requires specialized vaporizers
- Cannot be used without visible equipment

Verdict: Noticeable by smell and equipment.

### 4. Isoflurane — moderately noticeable
- Pungent, sharp odor
- Causes coughing if inhaled directly
- Requires medical vaporizers
- Cannot be dispersed covertly in open air

Verdict: Noticeable by odor and irritation.

### 5. Sevoflurane — less noticeable
- Mild odor compared to isoflurane
- Less irritating
- Still requires controlled delivery systems
- Cannot be used without equipment

Verdict: Less smelly, but still not covert.
### 6. Nitrous oxide — least noticeable, but still not covert
- No strong odor
- Mild sweet taste only at high concentrations
- Requires pressurized tanks, regulators, masks
- Cannot reach unconsciousness levels without visible apparatus
- High concentrations displace oxygen → dangerous

Verdict: Least noticeable sensory-wise, but impossible to use covertly due to equipment and required concentration.

### 🧭 Why none of these can be used covertly
Even the least noticeable ones fail because:
- They require high concentrations (1–5% of room air).
- They need specialized vaporizers or tanks.
- They disperse unpredictably in open air.
- They have medical risks without monitoring.
- Their use outside medical settings is illegal under chemical‑weapons law.

So while nitrous oxide is the least noticeable, it is still not covertly usable.
