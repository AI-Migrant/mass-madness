# Case Study: UK Authorities Destroying Evidence

## Question

What documented cases exist of UK authorities—including police forces, government departments, and other public bodies—destroying, concealing, or mishandling evidence, and what are the legal, systemic, and human consequences?

## Executive Summary

1. **Systemic Scale**: Over 30,000 prosecutions in England and Wales collapsed between October 2020 and September 2024 due to lost, damaged, or missing evidence, including 70 homicides and more than 550 sexual offences. This represents a 9% increase from 2020 to 2024, with evidence failures affecting approximately 1 in 20 Metropolitan Police prosecutions.
2. **Police Evidence Crisis**: A University of Leicester study found that 73% of lawyers surveyed had worked on criminal cases where police evidence was lost, destroyed, or contaminated. Ex-officers describe evidence storage as "overwhelming" and "chucked all over the place," with exhibits stored in broken fridges and under desks.
3. **Windrush Scandal**: The Home Office destroyed thousands of "landing cards" in October 2010—records that would have proven Windrush generation members' right to remain. The decision was taken in 2009 under Labour, but destruction occurred under the Conservative-Liberal Democrat coalition while Theresa May was Home Secretary. This was done under Data Protection Act obligations but became catastrophic when combined with the subsequent "hostile environment" policy.
4. **Legal Framework**: Destroying evidence can constitute perverting the course of justice (common law offence, maximum life imprisonment) or breaching Section 77 of the Freedom of Information Act 2000 (criminal offence when done to prevent disclosure).
5. **High-Profile Cases**: Essex Police detectives sabotaged child sex abuse investigations by destroying evidence and forging documents; Downing Street staff were allegedly instructed to delete WhatsApp messages during Partygate investigations; a Metropolitan Police inspector was accused of destroying evidence.
6. **Root Causes**: The 2012 closure of the Forensic Science Service, austerity cuts to police budgets, digital evidence explosion, court backlogs, and lack of specialist infrastructure for sensitive evidence (e.g., rape kits) have created a "perfect storm" of systemic failure.
7. **Human Impact**: Victims like Kiera, whose child sexual abuse interview was lost for nine years, face re-traumatization and delayed justice. Windrush victims lost homes, jobs, healthcare access, and were wrongly deported.

## Methodology

This research was conducted through iterative web searches focusing on:

- **Public information**: News investigations (BBC, The Independent, Channel 4), official reports (CPS data, HMICFRS inspections), academic studies (University of Leicester), and legal analysis
- **Source types**: Investigative journalism, Freedom of Information request data, parliamentary reports, legal guidance (CPS), NGO reports, and court documents
- **Search angles**: 
  - Scale and statistics of evidence loss/destruction
  - Specific high-profile cases (Windrush, Downing Street, Essex Police)
  - Legal framework and consequences
  - Systemic causes and institutional failures
  - Historical cases (Hillsborough, Orgreave)
  - Immigration authority cases

**Limitations**: 

- CPS E72 data does not break down specific reasons for case collapse
- Some cases remain under investigation with limited public information
- Internal police documents are not publicly accessible
- Historical cases may have incomplete digital records

## Findings

### 1. The Scale of the Problem: Systemic Evidence Failure

The criminal justice system is experiencing a crisis of evidence management that has reached unprecedented scale.

**BBC Investigation (June 2025)**: 

- More than 30,000 prosecutions collapsed between October 2020 and September 2024 due to evidence issues
- This includes 70 homicide cases and over 550 sexual offences
- The number of E72 codes (CPS classification for cases dropped due to evidential issues) increased by 9% from 2020 (7,484) to 2024 (8,180)
- Around 1 in 20 Metropolitan Police prosecutions were dropped due to missing evidence, compared to 1 in 50 across England and Wales

**University of Leicester Study**:

- 73% of lawyers surveyed had worked on cases where evidence was lost, destroyed, or contaminated
- Nearly half reported this happening on multiple occasions
- A 2021 Inside Justice study found 77% of police officers had seen evidence lost or destroyed

**Types of Evidence Being Lost**:

- Physical forensic evidence (DNA, fingerprints, weapons, clothing)
- Digital evidence (body-worn video, victim interview footage, CCTV)
- Witness statements and pathology reports
- Key evidence not gathered from crime scenes

### 2. Police Forces: Evidence Management Crisis

#### Case: Lancashire Police - Lost Child Sexual Abuse Evidence

- **Victim**: Kiera, sexually abused as a child
- **Failure**: Police lost her video interview recorded when she was 9 years old
- **Impact**: Delayed justice for 9 years; perpetrators jailed in October 2024 for nearly 30 years total
- **Systemic Issue**: Lancashire Police introduced new processes in 2015 to prevent recurrence, but national problems persist

#### Case: Essex Police - Sabotaging Child Sex Abuse Investigations

- **Officers**: Detective Constables Sharon Patterson (49) and Lee Pollard (47), who were in a relationship
- **Allegations**: 
  - Patterson falsely represented evidence to supervisors, fabricated witness statements
  - Created false CPS document to deceive supervisor
  - Pollard removed and destroyed four photographs that were "important exhibits"
  - Misrepresented evidence to prevent further action against suspects
- **Motivation**: Prosecutors alleged "laziness, self-preservation and a cynical disdain for complainants"
- **Impact**: Multiple child sex abuse investigations collapsed; allegations not properly investigated
- **Legal Status**: Both denied three counts of misconduct in a public office (2011-2014); trial ongoing

#### Case: Metropolitan Police - Evidence Destruction Allegation

- **Officer**: A Metropolitan Police inspector accused of destroying evidence
- **Context**: Part of broader concerns about Met Police evidence handling
- **Systemic Findings**: Baroness Casey's 2023 review found officers storing evidence in "over-stuffed, dilapidated or broken fridges and freezers containing evidence including the rape kits of victims"
- **Infrastructure Failure**: Review found "overworked and inexperienced workforce" lacked "infrastructure and specialism" for dealing with sexual offences after specialist unit was disbanded in 2019

#### Case: National Forensics Archive

- **Historical Context**: Forensic Science Service (FSS) closed in 2012 after making large losses
- **Current State**: National Forensics Archive (outside Birmingham) holds pre-2012 exhibits but accepts no new items
- **Impact**: Police forces had to create their own storage arrangements and contract private providers
- **Expert Assessment**: Prof. Carole McCartney (criminologist) witnessed an officer pull out a "box of horrors" containing uncatalogued evidence including a broken wine bottle and car number plate
- **Success Stories**: Archive exhibits were crucial in overturning wrongful convictions of Andrew Malkinson and Peter Sullivan

### 3. Government Departments: Deliberate Destruction

#### Case: Windrush Scandal - Home Office

**The Destruction**:

- **What**: Thousands of "landing cards" (registry slips) recording arrival dates of Caribbean migrants
- **When**: Decision made in June 2009 under Labour (Home Secretary Alan Johnson); actual destruction in October 2010 under Conservative-Lib Dem coalition (Home Secretary Theresa May)
- **Where**: Documents stored in basement of government tower block in Croydon; destroyed when office closed and staff moved
- **Why**: UK Border Agency claimed Data Protection Act 1998 obligations required disposal of personal information not kept "longer than necessary"

**The Impact**:

- **Immediate**: Loss of primary documentation proving arrival dates before 1971 Immigration Act cutoff
- **Escalation**: When "hostile environment" policy introduced in 2012, Windrush generation members could not prove their right to remain
- **Consequences**: 
  - Wrongful deportation of at least 83 people
  - Denial of healthcare, benefits, employment
  - Loss of homes and livelihoods
  - Families separated
  - At least 23 victims died before receiving compensation

**Whistleblower Revelations**:

- Former Home Office employee revealed staff had warned destruction would make it harder to verify Caribbean-born residents' status
- After 2013, requests from people wanting to know arrival dates increased dramatically
- Standard response became: "We have searched our records, we can find no trace of you in our files"

**Legal and Political Fallout**:

- Home Office initially claimed documents were of "limited value" and did not provide "reliable evidence relating to ongoing residence"
- Independent review (Wendy Williams, 2020) concluded Home Office showed "ignorance and thoughtlessness"; scandal was "foreseeable and avoidable"
- Amber Rudd resigned as Home Secretary in April 2018
- Compensation scheme announced but by November 2021, only \~5% of victims had received payments

**Expert Assessment**:

- Immigration barristers Damian Krushner and Adrian Berry: Landing cards recorded arrival dates, travel origins, and were "first official UK record of a person's existence"
- Cards could establish foundation for proving continuous UK residence when combined with other evidence
- Destruction alone might not have caused scandal without subsequent hostile environment policy

#### Case: Downing Street - Partygate Evidence Deletion

**The Allegations**:

- **Sources**: Two sources claimed senior Downing Street staff advised them to "clean up" their phones
- **Instruction**: Remove messages implying attendance at or awareness of gatherings that could "look like a party"
- **Context**: During Cabinet Office investigation into alleged lockdown gatherings

**Legal Warnings**:

- **Information Commissioner's Office (ICO)**: "Erasing, destroying or concealing information within scope of a Freedom of Information request, with the intention of preventing its disclosure is a criminal offence under section 77 of the Freedom of Information Act"
- **Campaign Groups**: The Citizens and Foxglove sent legal letter warning that deleting WhatsApp messages would break the law
- **Foxglove Director Cori Crider**: "There's little point in holding any inquiry if bosses make staff delete key evidence the moment it's announced"

**Government Response**:

- Downing Street: "Do not recognise" the claims; staff given "clear guidance to retain any relevant information"
- Investigation limitations: Personal phones cannot be accessed without staff volunteering them; Sue Gray had no legal power to compel evidence from MPs and ministers

### 4. Other Public Bodies

#### Post Office Horizon Scandal

- **Context**: While primarily a miscarriage of justice involving faulty software, the scandal involved evidence handling failures
- **Allegations**: Post Office failed to disclose knowledge of Horizon system faults during criminal and civil cases
- **Impact**: Over 900 subpostmasters wrongfully convicted based on faulty data
- **Investigation**: Metropolitan Police opened investigation into Post Office and Fujitsu personnel
- **Recent Development**: UK Post Office executive suspended over allegations of destroying software scandal evidence (October 2024)

### 5. Legal Framework and Consequences

#### Criminal Offences

**Perverting the Course of Justice (Common Law)**:

- **Definition**: Doing an act that has a tendency to, and is intended to, interfere with the administration of justice
- **Maximum Sentence**: Life imprisonment
- **Court**: Trial on indictment only (Crown Court)
- **Examples Relevant to Evidence Destruction**:
  - Destroying evidence or exhibits
  - Concealing evidence
  - Fabricating or altering evidence
  - Interfering with witnesses to give false evidence or alter statements
  - Giving false information to police to frustrate inquiries

**CPS Guidance**:

- Offence committed when accused does an act or series of acts that has/have a tendency to pervert and is/are intended to pervert the course of public justice
- Course of justice must be in existence at time of acts
- Includes civil and criminal justice
- "The offence is committed when acts tending and intended to pervert a course of justice are done" - success not required

**Freedom of Information Act 2000 - Section 77**:

- **Offence**: Erasing, destroying, or concealing information within scope of an FOI request with intention of preventing disclosure
- **Penalty**: Criminal offence
- **Context**: Applies to public authorities and their staff

#### Civil Liability

**Legal Rights for Victims**:

- Difficult to sue police for negligence due to significant legal protections
- **Human Rights Act Claims**: More viable legal path
  - Right to a fair trial (Article 6 ECHR)
  - Right not to be subjected to inhuman or degrading treatment (Article 3 ECHR)
- **Example**: Woman whose rape case collapsed due to police failing to collect forensic evidence received £15,000 compensation under Human Rights Act (no liability admitted)

### 6. Systemic Causes

**Institutional Factors**:

1. **Forensic Science Service Closure (2012)**:
  - All police forces previously sent exhibits to government-owned FSS for storage and analysis
  - Closure forced police to create their own arrangements and contract private providers
  - Loss of centralized expertise and infrastructure
2. **Austerity Cuts**:
  - Police budget cuts throughout 2010s reduced resources for evidence storage and management
  - Leading criminologist: Increase in evidence failures is "largely a resourcing issue"
3. **Digital Evidence Explosion**:
  - HMICFRS (2022): Police forces "struggling to meet the demands placed on it by the digital age"
  - Growth in online crime and digital evidence (body-worn video, CCTV, mobile phone data)
  - Backlogs at courts adding to evidence volume
4. **Infrastructure Failures**:
  - Broken storage facilities (fridges, freezers for biological evidence)
  - Lack of specialist units (Met Police disbanded sexual offences specialist unit in 2019)
  - Evidence stored inappropriately (under desks, in boxes of "horrors")
5. **Cultural Issues**:
  - Baroness Casey review: "Overworked and inexperienced workforce"
  - Essex Police case: "Cynical disdain for complainants"
  - Lack of accountability and oversight

### 7. Historical Context

**Hillsborough Disaster (1989)**:

- Police altered witness statements to shift blame from authorities to Liverpool fans
- Taylor Report and subsequent investigations revealed systemic manipulation of evidence
- 97 football fans died; families fought for decades for justice

**Orgreave Miners' Strike (1984)**:

- Allegations that South Yorkshire Police destroyed or fabricated evidence
- Independent Police Complaints Commission investigation ongoing for years
- Part of broader pattern of police misconduct in industrial disputes

**Conflicts and Caveats**:

- Home Office claims Windrush landing cards were of "limited value" and destruction was required by Data Protection Act; immigration lawyers dispute this assessment
- Police forces argue not all E72 cases relate to lost evidence; some involve inability to find expert witnesses or obtain medical statements
- The actual decision-maker for Windrush document destruction remains disputed between Labour (2009 decision) and Conservative-Lib Dem coalition (2010 implementation)

## Open Questions

1. **Accountability**: Who specifically authorized the destruction of Windrush landing cards, and were they aware of the potential consequences?
2. **Full Scale**: What is the complete number of prosecutions affected by evidence destruction across all UK jurisdictions, including Scotland and Northern Ireland?
3. **Digital Evidence**: How much digital evidence (body-worn video, CCTV, mobile data) is being permanently lost due to storage limitations and retention policies?
4. **Systemic Solutions**: Will the Law Commission's proposal to re-establish a national forensic service and criminalize evidence mishandling be implemented?
5. **Compensation**: How many Windrush victims have received compensation, and is the scheme adequate given the harm caused?
6. **Cultural Change**: What specific measures are being taken to address the cultural issues identified in Baroness Casey's review and other investigations?
7. **Prevention**: Are current police evidence storage and management protocols sufficient to prevent future large-scale evidence loss?

## Recommendations and Next Steps

### Immediate Actions

1. **National Forensic Service**: Re-establish a centralized, properly funded forensic service to restore expertise and infrastructure lost since 2012
2. **Legislative Reform**: Pass laws making deliberate destruction or mishandling of evidence a specific criminal offence with clear penalties
3. **Resource Investment**: Increase funding for police evidence storage, digital forensics, and specialist units (particularly for sexual offences)
4. **Accountability Mechanisms**: Strengthen independent oversight of evidence handling with real consequences for failures

### For Victims

1. **Legal Advice**: Victims of evidence destruction should seek specialist legal advice regarding potential Human Rights Act claims
2. **Documentation**: Maintain personal records and copies of any evidence provided to authorities
3. **Support Services**: Access victim support organizations for emotional and practical assistance

### For Researchers and Journalists

1. **FOI Requests**: Continue pursuing Freedom of Information requests for detailed breakdowns of E72 cases by cause and police force
2. **Whistleblower Protection**: Establish secure channels for public sector whistleblowers to report evidence destruction
3. **Longitudinal Studies**: Track the impact of any implemented reforms on evidence loss rates over time

### For Policymakers

1. **Evidence Retention Standards**: Develop and enforce national standards for evidence retention across all police forces
2. **Digital Infrastructure**: Invest in digital evidence management systems capable of handling the volume and complexity of modern investigations
3. **Training**: Implement comprehensive training programs on evidence handling for all police officers and staff
4. **Cultural Reform**: Address the cultural issues identified in reviews through leadership, accountability, and support for officers
