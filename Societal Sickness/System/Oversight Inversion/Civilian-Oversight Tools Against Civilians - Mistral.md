# How Authorities Worldwide Repurpose Civilian-Oversight Tools Against Civilians

## Question

How do authorities worldwide use tools and mechanisms that are supposed to enable civilian oversight — of policing, intelligence, public order, and the state — in ways that instead act against civilians and civil society?

## Executive Summary

1. Civilian oversight of policing is widely undermined through structural disarmament: oversight boards are created but denied investigative or disciplinary authority, allowing authorities to claim accountability while neutralising its effects. A NACOLE survey found 38 of 64 oversight bodies lacked independent investigative power; in Berkeley in 2026 a police chief rejected all 13 sustained misconduct findings.

2. Body-worn cameras, sold as a transparency and accountability mechanism, have been operationally inverted: a 2016 study found 92.6% of prosecutors in body-cam jurisdictions used the footage to prosecute civilians, while only 8.3% used it against officers. Police control what is recorded, withheld, and released.

3. Surveillance laws framed as targeting foreign threats are routinely turned inward. FISA Section 702, limited to foreigners abroad, swept in Americans' communications; the FBI ran an estimated 3.4 million warrantless "backdoor searches" of U.S. persons in 2021 alone.

4. Counter-terrorism and public-order legislation is repurposed to criminalise peaceful dissent: the UK Terrorism Act 2000 has been used against over 2,000 people for expressing support for a proscribed protest group; the UK's Public Order Act 2023 produced custodial sentences for protest tactics that previously drew fines.

5. Emergency powers (notably during COVID-19) became vehicles for "opportunistic repression": peer-reviewed research documents states using public-health enforcement to target civilians, opposition, and journalists beyond any health rationale.

6. Commercial spyware marketed for fighting crime and terrorism — above all NSO Group's Pegasus — has been deployed against journalists, activists, opposition politicians, and judges across India, Hungary, Mexico, and elsewhere. India's Supreme Court has not compelled the government to account for alleged use.

7. "Foreign agent" and "fake news" laws, and national-security statutes (e.g. Hong Kong's NSL), rebrand transparency and oversight actors — NGOs, journalists, protesters — as security threats, forcing closure of over 100 organisations and chilling independent scrutiny.

8. Privacy and data-protection frameworks can be inverted into weapons: GDPR-based SLAPPs and data-protection threats are used to silence investigative journalism that relies on personal data.

## Methodology

The investigation drew on web search across five thematic clusters: (1) police accountability / civilian review boards; (2) surveillance and intelligence oversight; (3) counter-terrorism, public-order, and emergency powers; (4) spyware and surveillance technology; (5) legal-regulatory tools (foreign-agent, disinformation, data-protection laws). Source types included peer-reviewed academic articles, investigative journalism (Washington Post, ProPublica, Forbidden Stories), legal-analysis bodies (Brennan Center, ICNL, CIMA), international NGOs (Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, FIDH, Open Society Foundations), UN/OHCHR material, and primary legislative references. Where full pages were blocked, claims were corroborated from snippets and adjacent sources. Limitations: the review is English-language-source dependent; several fast-moving jurisdictions (China, Russia interior, Gulf states) are under-represented; quantitative claims rely on the cited estimates and may not reflect the most recent reporting period.

## Findings

### 1. Civilian Oversight Boards: Accountability Theatre

Civilian review boards (CRBs) exist to give non-police actors input into misconduct investigations and policy. In practice they are frequently designed to be unable to act:

- NACOLE survey (via Washington Post): of 64 responding oversight groups, 38 had no independent investigative authority and were limited to auditing internal-affairs work; 34 had no authority even to recommend discipline.
- Berkeley, 2026: the oversight board sustained 13 misconduct allegations; the police chief rejected all of them.
- New York CCRB: research showed a model where police leadership could decline to discipline an officer after a substantiated allegation, blunting the board's impact.
- Albuquerque: a federal consent decree demanded a stronger oversight agency, but the police union sued to block it, the city council took four years to grant subpoena power, and the agency completed reviews of only 5 of 27 fatal shootings since 2015 — exonerating officers in all five.
- Scholars and former oversight leaders (Sharon Fairley, University of Chicago) note that civilian oversight is "not a panacea" without power and resources.

Pattern: the mechanism is established to demonstrate responsiveness, then structurally hollowed out so that it cannot constrain the authority it oversees, while absorbing community demand for accountability.

### 2. Body-Worn Cameras: The "Transparency Charade"

Body cameras were introduced as the largest new investment in policing in a generation, promising accountability. ProPublica documents a systemic inversion:

- A 2016 study found 92.6% of prosecutors' offices in body-camera jurisdictions used footage as evidence against private citizens, while 8.3% used it to prosecute officers.
- Police departments retain control over what is recorded, what is released, and when — framing the narrative after incidents (e.g. the NYPD's selective release after the 2017 shooting of Miguel Richards).
- Policing-oversight expert Hans Menos coined the term "transparency charade" for the recurring pattern of public transparency talk followed by deliberate undermining of the stated goal.
- EFF and ACLU warn that without strong access and retention policies, body cameras become "just another police surveillance tool" used against the communities they were meant to protect.

### 3. Surveillance and Intelligence Oversight Inverted

Tools framed as oversight of, or safeguards against, mass surveillance are repeatedly turned on civilians:

- FISA Section 702 (United States): authorises warrantless surveillance only of foreigners abroad, but inevitably sweeps in Americans' communications. The FBI, CIA, NSA, and NCTC conduct thousands of warrantless "backdoor searches" (U.S.-person queries) of the resulting database yearly — an estimated 3.4 million in 2021. Civil-liberties groups call this a "bait and switch" that pierces Fourth Amendment protections.
- Border-surveillance technology: Reason and POGO report that surveillance systems built and legally justified for border control are being redeployed against Americans inland, expanding the surveillance state beyond its statutory justification.
- Congressional intelligence oversight itself is structurally weak (Zegart, Hoover Institution): members gain no constituency benefit from intelligence committee service, producing "benign neglect" and limited constraint on the intelligence community — so the oversight layer that should protect civilians under-functions.

### 4. Counter-Terrorism and Public-Order Laws Against Protesters

Anti-terrorism and public-order frameworks, justified as protecting civilians, are used to criminalise civilian dissent:

- United Kingdom: the Terrorism Act 2000 has been applied against over 2,000 people for expressing support for the proscribed group Palestine Action, mostly under the Section 12 offence of supporting a proscribed organisation. HRW and UN experts criticise proscription of a direct-action protest group as conflating protest with terrorism.
- UK Public Order Act 2023 / PCSCA 2022: vague thresholds such as "serious disruption to the life of the community" give police broad discretion. HRW's 2026 report documents arbitrary arrests, criminal charges for peaceful activity, and rising custodial sentences. Just Stop Oil co-founder Roger Hallam was sentenced to five years; the Constitution Society analyses proportionality concerns in 2024 protest sentencing.
- United States: the ICNL US Protest Law Tracker records state bills creating new protest felonies (e.g. "subversion") tied to Foreign Terrorist Organization designations, channelling anti-terrorism architecture into protest policing.
- LSE analysis traces a global trend of applying terror laws to non-violent protesters.

### 5. Emergency Powers as Opportunistic Repression

COVID-19 emergency powers became a pretext for civilian targeting beyond any public-health need:

- MIT Press (International Security): peer-reviewed study of "Opportunistic Repression" finds states used pandemic enforcement — where nonviolent, proportionate measures (fines) were feasible — to target civilians, opposition, and dissent.
- UN High Commissioner for Human Rights (2020): "emergency powers should not be a weapon governments can wield" against populations.
- Freedom House "Democracy under Lockdown": officials and security services perpetrated violence against civilians, detained people without justification, and granted themselves powers beyond what public health required.
- Foreign Policy and The Conversation document the silencing of dissent and "quiet repurposing" of public data (health, mobility, financial) collected for pandemic response into general surveillance infrastructure.

### 6. Commercial Spyware Against Civilians

NSO Group markets Pegasus as a tool for fighting crime and terrorism; governments have routinely used it against civilians:

- Pegasus Project (2021, Forbidden Stories/Amnesty): revealed government espionage on journalists, opposition politicians, activists, lawyers, and business figures worldwide.
- India: alleged targeting of ministers, opposition leaders, journalists, activists, Supreme Court judges, and election commissioners. Amnesty (2023) forensically confirmed repeated targeting of high-profile journalists. The Supreme Court established a technical committee but, per critics, has not compelled the government to account for deployment — legitimising spying on critics.
- Rest of World: governments from Russia to India to Iran use facial-recognition technology to locate and surveil protesters. A 2026 U.S. class action challenges DHS photographing, scanning, and recording protesters' biometric and licence-plate data after they documented immigration arrests in public spaces.
- OHCHR (2022): UN report warns spyware and surveillance constitute growing threats to privacy and human rights.

### 7. Legal Tools That Relabel Oversight Actors as Threats

Several frameworks reframe the very civilians and organisations that perform oversight functions as security risks:

- Foreign-agent laws: Russia's 2012 law (model now replicated) labels NGOs and media receiving foreign funding as "foreign agents," discrediting and choking funding; Nicaragua deregistered over 150 organisations; Georgia-style and other variants force closures. HRW calls these part of an "authoritarian playbook" that stigmatises independent civil society as a "trojan horse."
- Disinformation / "fake news" laws: CIMA and IBA document that laws nominally combating misinformation are "typically designed to undermine the free flow of information and investigative reporting, to undermine dissent and criticism of government." IPI records a rush of such laws during COVID-19.
- Hong Kong National Security Law: Georgetown Law and ISHR document the use of the NSL and related tools to force closure of over 100 NGOs and media outlets, arresting at least 44 civil-society and media figures, sparing no sector (opposition parties, journalists, lawyers, activists, academics). HRW calls the 2024 Safeguarding National Security Ordinance a "full-scale assault on rights" punishing peaceful speech with heavy prison sentences.

### 8. Data-Protection and Privacy Frameworks Weaponised

Even rights-protective frameworks can be inverted:

- GDPR-based SLAPPs: the American Journal of International Law (Cambridge) and Taylor & Francis legal scholars document private individuals weaponising data-protection law to "threaten, silence, or hinder journalistic stories that rely on or include personal data," creating a fertile ground for strategic litigation against public participation in states where the journalistic exemption is narrow.
- The result: a transparency mechanism (data-protection rights) becomes a litigation lever against the journalists and watchdogs who provide civilian oversight of power.

## Open Questions

- What proportion of oversight-board "sustained" findings are ultimately overturned or ignored by chiefs and arbitrators nationally, and what accountability follows?
- How extensive is the repurposing of pandemic-era data infrastructure into permanent surveillance systems, and which jurisdictions have rolled it back?
- What is the full scope of Pegasus deployment beyond documented cases, and how many governments have purchased equivalent commercial spyware?
- Does data-protection litigation against journalism produce measurable chilling effects comparable to classic SLAPPs, and across which EU member states?
- Where civilian oversight bodies have retained real disciplinary power, what design features account for their effectiveness?

## Recommendations / Next Steps

- For policymakers: grant oversight bodies independent investigative authority and binding disciplinary power, or they risk functioning as accountability theatre; remove police-union and chief veto over findings.
- For legislators: insert "use limits" and sunset clauses into emergency, counter-terrorism, and surveillance laws so powers cannot drift from their stated purpose to ordinary protest policing.
- For civil society and oversight actors: audit body-camera and surveillance data flows to track how footage and datasets are used — against officers versus against civilians — and publish the asymmetry.
- For courts: require governments to account for spyware procurement and deployment against citizens (as India's Supreme Court has been urged to do), and to test public-order and proscription measures against necessity and proportionality.
- For researchers: build a comparative dataset mapping each oversight mechanism to documented instances of its inversion against civilians, to distinguish design flaws from deliberate repurposing.
