# How Companies in the UK Work with Authorities Despite Lacking Legal Basis

## Question

How do companies in the United Kingdom collaborate with government authorities (including the Home Office, police forces, intelligence agencies, and regulators) through mechanisms that lack explicit statutory basis or where cooperation extends beyond legal obligations?

## Executive Summary

1. **Informal Agreements Override Statute**: UK authorities frequently use Memorandums of Understanding (MoUs) and other non-statutory arrangements to compel or encourage private sector cooperation, bypassing parliamentary scrutiny. The NHS-Home Office patient data sharing MoU (2016-2018) exemplifies this—completely withdrawn after legal challenge as it had no clear statutory footing.

2. **Voluntary Compliance as Default**: In sectors like banking and telecoms, companies often cooperate beyond legal requirements. Banks receive Home Office data on "disqualified persons" but face **no legal obligation** to check customers against it; telecoms (EE, Vodafone, Three) automated data sharing with police via RIPA, while only O2 requires staff review.

3. **Unlawful Intelligence Collection**: The Investigatory Powers Tribunal ruled that MI5, MI6, and GCHQ **unlawfully collected** bulk communications data on Privacy International. GCHQ has accessed private companies' databases since 2001, and intelligence agencies share data with private sector partners without sufficient controls.

4. **Hostile Environment's Private Sector Enforcement**: The Home Office's "Hostile Environment" policy outsources immigration enforcement to banks, landlords, employers, and the NHS through a mix of statutory duties (Right to Rent) and informal data sharing, creating systemic discrimination risks.

5. **Facial Recognition's Lawless Expansion**: Police and private companies (e.g., Argent at King's Cross) deployed live facial recognition without specific legal authority. Courts found South Wales Police's use **unlawful** in 2020, yet expansion continues with £50m Home Office funding for a centralised biometric platform.

6. **Mass Surveillance Infrastructure**: Telecoms provide automated access to customer records (100,000+ requests/year), intelligence agencies access telecoms' databases, and airlines share PNR data under derogations that expand beyond original terrorism/serious crime purposes.

7. **Legal Challenges Force Retreats, But Not Structural Change**: Courts have struck down specific arrangements (NHS MoU, Right to Rent scheme, facial recognition deployments), but authorities reformulate programs rather than establish clear statutory frameworks.

## Findings

### 1. Memorandums of Understanding: The Shadow Legal Framework

**NHS-Home Office Patient Data Sharing (2016-2018)**

The most documented case of cooperation lacking clear legal basis involved a **secret MoU** between NHS Digital, the Department of Health, and the Home Office, established in November 2016. This arrangement allowed the Home Office to request non-clinical patient information (last known address, date of birth) for immigration enforcement purposes.

- **No statutory basis**: The MoU was an administrative agreement, not grounded in specific legislation authorizing such data sharing for immigration purposes.
- **Legal challenge**: Migrants' Rights Network (MRN), represented by Liberty, mounted a legal challenge arguing the agreement violated patient confidentiality, discriminated against non-British patients, and deterred migrants from seeking healthcare.
- **Government retreat**: Under pressure, the government **suspended** the arrangement in May 2018, limiting it to serious crime cases. After continued legal pressure, NHS Digital **completely withdrew** from the MoU on 2 November 2018.
- **Court findings**: The arrangement was found to violate patients' right to privacy under the Human Rights Act and failed the public interest test required to breach doctor-patient confidentiality.

**Banking Sector Data Sharing**

While the Immigration Acts 2014 and 2016 provide a statutory framework for restricting bank accounts of disqualified persons, the implementation relies heavily on **voluntary cooperation**:

- Home Office provides banks with data on individuals known to be in the UK unlawfully
- **Banks are not legally required** to check customers' documents or act on this data
- Banks voluntarily check their customer databases against Home Office-provided lists
- If matches are found, banks notify the Home Office, which then issues instructions (from no action to account closure)
- Data sharing was **paused in 2018** after the Windrush scandal, then **resumed in April 2023** with claimed safeguards

### 2. Intelligence Agencies: Unlawful Collection from Private Sector

**Privacy International v. Intelligence Agencies (2018)**

In a landmark case, the Investigatory Powers Tribunal (IPT) ruled that UK intelligence agencies unlawfully collected data on Privacy International:

- **MI5**: Accessed and examined bulk communications data and bulk personal data relating to Privacy International **unlawfully**
- **GCHQ and MI6**: Also collected bulk communications data and bulk personal data about Privacy International **unlawfully**
- **Data sources**: GCHQ has collected data from **telecoms and internet companies** since 2001; MI5 since 2005
- **Sharing practices**: Intelligence services share huge datasets about largely innocent people with:
  - Foreign intelligence services (Five Eyes network)
  - **Private sector partners**
  - Other UK government agencies (HMRC, etc.)
  - **Without sufficient controls** on how the data will be used

**GCHQ-NSA Intelligence Sharing**

The IPT also ruled that GCHQ acted unlawfully in accessing millions of people's personal communications collected by the NSA. Secret policies revealed that British intelligence services can request or receive access to bulk data from foreign agencies without a warrant whenever it would "not be technically feasible" for the government to obtain it themselves.

### 3. Telecoms: Automated Mass Surveillance

**RIPA and Beyond**

The Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 (RIPA) provides a legal framework, but implementation reveals extensive voluntary cooperation:

- **EE, Vodafone, Three**: Have **automated systems** to handle all information requests made by police through RIPA
- **O2**: The **only major network** requiring staff to review all police information requests
- **Scale**: Approximately **100,000 information requests** made every year through RIPA, mainly by police but also by other public authorities including councils
- **Criticism**: The relationship between telecoms and law enforcement described as "unquestioning" with automated access creating a de facto state database of private communications

**Telecoms Fraud Charter**

A **voluntary charter** signed by BT EE, Virgin Media O2, Vodafone Three, Sky, TalkTalk, and others commits to:
- Building **scalable, collaborative data-sharing models** between telecoms, banks, and tech firms
- Developing call tracing technology to track fraudulent calls across networks
- Providing police with information to dismantle scam operations
- This represents **cooperation beyond legal requirements**

### 4. Facial Recognition: Private Sector as Surveillance Infrastructure

**Police-Private Company Collusion**

Big Brother Watch documentation reveals extensive collaboration between police forces and private companies in deploying facial recognition surveillance:

- **Argent at King's Cross**: Private property developer installed CCTV with facial recognition **without public consultation**
- **Watchlist composition**: Police use images from:
  - Police-originated sources (custody images)
  - **Non-police-originated images** (publicly available, open source)
  - Information **shared by other public bodies**
- **Legal status**: **No specific law** regulates facial recognition in the UK
- **Court ruling**: South Wales Police's use found **unlawful** by Court of Appeal in 2020
- **Expansion continues**: Home Office allocated **£50 million** for a centralised facial matching platform integrating law enforcement and immigration databases

**Retail and Public Spaces**

- Schools have rolled out facial recognition-powered cashless canteens
- Some charge children for a swipecard if they don't consent to biometric scans
- Police-funded studies found **significant racial biases** in facial recognition algorithms
- **Parliament has yet to pass any law** banning or regulating live facial recognition

### 5. Hostile Environment: Outsourcing Immigration Enforcement

**Banking Sector**

The Home Office's banking measures, part of the Hostile Environment policy:
- **No requirement on banks**: Banks are not legally required to check customers' documents
- **Voluntary checking**: Banks receive Home Office data and check against it voluntarily
- **Impact**: Prevents people with irregular status from opening/operating current accounts
- **Safeguards**: Age restriction applied in 2018 (only those born after 1 Jan 1989), later expanded
- **Suspension**: Paused in 2018 due to Windrush scandal, resumed April 2023

**Right to Rent Scheme**

While the Immigration Act 2014 created a legal obligation for landlords to check immigration status:
- **Discrimination effect**: Landlords avoid renting to anyone without a UK passport to minimize risk
- **Legal challenge**: Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants (JCWI) successfully challenged the scheme
- **High Court ruling (2018)**: Scheme found **incompatible with human rights** as it causes discrimination
- **Court finding**: Scheme "does not merely provide the occasion or opportunity for private landlords to discriminate but **causes them to do so** where otherwise they would not"
- **Appeal**: Court of Appeal later overturned the human rights incompatibility finding, but the scheme remains controversial

**NHS Reference Numbers**

After the MoU withdrawal, new plans emerged to:
- Assign **Home Office reference numbers to migrants' NHS files**
- This enables Home Office to track migrants' healthcare records
- Does not aid NHS in identifying fee status (they already know who paid the Immigration Health Surcharge)
- **Same effect**: Singles out migrantic patients, potentially deterring them from seeking treatment

### 6. Airlines and Passenger Data

**PNR Data Collection**

Passenger Name Record (PNR) data collection is mandated by law, but with concerning expansions:
- Airlines must provide PNR data using standardized formats (PNRGOV EDIFACT)
- UK has **derogations** from EU safeguards for automated surveillance and profiling
- UK processes PNR data for purposes beyond terrorism/serious crime in "exceptional cases"
- UK-EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement allows expanded processing: against departure confirmation, nationality, residence information, then against dynamic multi-criteria risk assessment tools

### Conflicts and Caveats

- **Legal vs. Lawful**: Some cooperation occurs under legal frameworks (RIPA, Immigration Acts) but implementation exceeds what is strictly lawful or proportionate
- **Secrecy**: Many arrangements remain undisclosed; information emerges through legal challenges and FOI requests
- **Evolving safeguards**: Authorities often add safeguards after legal challenges, but these may not address structural issues
- **Private sector motivation**: Companies cooperate due to pressure, reputation management, or commercial interests, not just legal obligation

## Open Questions

1. **Full extent of MoUs**: How many other non-statutory data sharing agreements exist between UK authorities and private companies across all sectors?

2. **Intelligence agency private sector access**: What specific private companies provide data to GCHQ, MI5, and MI6, and under what arrangements?

3. **Telecoms automated sharing**: What proportion of the 100,000 annual RIPA requests are processed automatically without human review, and what errors result?

4. **Facial recognition expansion**: How many private companies are currently deploying facial recognition for police or their own purposes without clear legal authority?

5. **Banking sector impact**: How many bank account closures have resulted from the Home Office data sharing program, and what proportion were erroneous?

6. **PNR data uses**: How is the UK using its derogations from EU PNR safeguards in practice, and what "exceptional cases" justify expanded processing?

7. **Right to Rent discrimination**: What is the current scale of discrimination in private renting due to the Right to Rent scheme, and has it decreased since the 2018 ruling?

## Recommendations / Next Steps

### For Policymakers
- **Legislate or cease**: Require all data sharing between authorities and private sector to have explicit statutory basis with parliamentary oversight
- **Transparency**: Mandate public registration of all MoUs and data sharing agreements
- **Oversight**: Strengthen independent oversight of intelligence agency access to private sector data
- **Proportionality**: Ensure any cooperation is necessary, proportionate, and subject to judicial authorization

### For Companies
- **Legal review**: Conduct independent legal reviews of all data sharing with authorities to ensure compliance with GDPR and human rights law
- **Transparency reports**: Publish annual transparency reports on government data requests and sharing
- **Resist overreach**: Challenge requests that lack clear legal basis or are disproportionate
- **Human rights impact**: Assess human rights impacts of cooperation with immigration enforcement

### For Civil Society
- **Freedom of Information**: Continue FOI requests to uncover undisclosed agreements
- **Legal challenges**: Pursue strategic litigation against unlawful cooperation mechanisms
- **Public awareness**: Educate the public on their rights regarding private sector data sharing
- **Corporate accountability**: Pressure companies through shareholder activism and public campaigns

### For Researchers
- **Mapping exercise**: Systematically map all known data sharing mechanisms between UK authorities and private sector
- **Sector comparisons**: Compare practices across banking, healthcare, telecoms, tech, transport
- **Impact assessment**: Quantify the human impact of voluntary cooperation mechanisms
- **International comparison**: Compare UK practices with other jurisdictions to identify best practices
