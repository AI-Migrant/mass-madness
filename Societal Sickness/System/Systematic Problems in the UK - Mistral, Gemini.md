### 1. Use of Army PsyOps to Monitor Citizens
#### The 77th Brigade
The UK government was exposed for using the **77th Brigade**, a military psychological operations (PsyOps) unit, to monitor British citizens on social media. This directly contradicted official statements in January 2021 that the 77th Brigade had never targeted UK citizens. The operation also involved spreading misinformation, treating citizen-victims as foreigners, and considering embedding government censors within social media platforms.

The 77th Brigade, formed in 2015, is a unit of the British Army that specializes in psychological operations, media operations, and cyber warfare. Originally presented as a force for countering foreign disinformation and propaganda, investigative reports revealed that the unit was deployed to monitor domestic social media activity, including tracking British citizens who were critical of government policies. Documents obtained through Freedom of Information requests showed that the Brigade used fake social media personas to engage with UK citizens, monitor online discussions, and in some cases, disseminate government-approved narratives. The deception extended to parliamentary statements, where ministers explicitly denied that the 77th Brigade targeted UK citizens, only for these claims to be later disproven by leaked internal communications. The operation also explored partnerships with social media platforms to embed government representatives directly within their content moderation teams, raising concerns about state censorship and the erosion of free speech.

This case highlights government deception, surveillance overreach, and the misuse of military resources for domestic monitoring, raising serious concerns about transparency and accountability.

### 2. Government Secrecy and State Impunity
#### Northern Ireland Troubles Cases
The UK Supreme Court ruled in favor of the government's appeal to block the disclosure of sensitive material in an inquest over the 1994 murder of Liam Paul Thompson, a case linked to the Troubles. The government argued that releasing the information would pose a national security risk, but critics, including Amnesty International, condemned the ruling as a blank cheque to conceal state wrongdoing and human rights violations.

The case of Liam Paul Thompson is emblematic of a broader pattern of state impunity in Northern Ireland. Thompson, a Catholic civilian, was murdered in 1994 in circumstances that allegedly involved collusion between loyalist paramilitaries and state security forces. Despite repeated calls from victims' families and human rights organizations for full transparency, the UK government has consistently resisted disclosing documents related to the case, citing national security concerns. The Supreme Court's decision to uphold the government's appeal sets a dangerous precedent, effectively allowing the state to withhold evidence indefinitely. This has been criticized as a mechanism to protect security personnel from accountability, even in cases involving extrajudicial killings. Amnesty International and other advocacy groups argue that such secrecy undermines the rule of law and perpetuates a culture of impunity, where state agents can act without fear of consequences. The ruling also raises questions about the UK's compliance with its international human rights obligations, particularly under the European Convention on Human Rights, which requires effective investigations into allegations of state involvement in violent crimes.

This case underscores government secrecy, impunity for state agents, and the suppression of truth in historical conflicts.

### 3. Safeguarding Failures in Child Care Centres
Multiple cases of **child sexual abuse in nurseries** have exposed systemic failures in safeguarding. For example:
- **Vincent Chan**, a nursery worker, was jailed for 18 years in February 2026 for 56 counts of abuse, including **molesting children as young as three**. Chan's crimes spanned over a decade, with authorities failing to intervene despite his history of predatory behavior.
- *Nathan Bennett*, another nursery worker, was convicted of **rape and sexual assault** against five boys aged two and three. His crimes were only discovered after a nursery manager reviewed CCTV footage.

These cases are not isolated incidents but part of a disturbing pattern of institutional failure. In the Vincent Chan case, concerns about his behavior had been raised multiple times by colleagues and parents, yet these warnings were either ignored or inadequately investigated by local authorities and Ofsted, the UK's education regulator. Chan was able to move between different childcare settings without proper vetting, exploiting gaps in the safeguarding system. Similarly, Nathan Bennett's abuse went undetected for years, with the nursery in question failing to implement basic safeguarding protocols, such as regular CCTV reviews and proper staff supervision. The discovery of Bennett's crimes only occurred by chance, when a nursery manager decided to review footage after noticing suspicious behavior. These failures highlight a culture of complacency within childcare institutions, where concerns are often brushed aside or not taken seriously. The lack of accountability for these failures has eroded public trust in the safeguarding system, with many parents now questioning the safety of their children in institutional care.

These cases reveal institutional impunity, inadequate oversight, and a culture of brushing concerns aside, leading to prolonged abuse.

### 4. Judicial and Executive Overreach
Executive overreach which even courts have started to intervene.

#### Misuse of prerogative powers
The UK executive has increasingly relied on the royal prerogative of mercy and other prerogative powers to bypass parliamentary scrutiny. This includes the use of executive orders to implement significant policy changes without parliamentary debate or approval. For example, the government has used prerogative powers to deploy military forces, enter into international treaties, and grant pardons without consulting Parliament. Such actions undermine the principle of parliamentary sovereignty and reduce the ability of elected representatives to hold the executive to account.

##### Military deployment without parliamentary approval
###### Case: R (Gentle) v Prime Minister [2008] UKHL 20

**Facts**: Mothers of soldiers killed in Iraq challenged the legality of the 2003 invasion, arguing the Government failed to satisfy itself that the war was lawful under international law before deploying troops under the royal prerogative.

**Significance**: The House of Lords ruled that while the decision to go to war is in principle susceptible to judicial review under the Human Rights Act 1998, the courts would not second-guess the executive’s assessment of international law. The case exposed the lack of parliamentary oversight in military deployments, as the Government relied solely on the royal prerogative (exercised by the Prime Minister) without a Commons vote.

###### Case: Chandler v Director of Public Prosecutions [1964] AC 763

**Facts**: Anti-nuclear protesters were prosecuted under the Official Secrets Act 1911 after entering an RAF station to obstruct military aircraft. They argued that their actions were intended to prevent a dangerous and unlawful foreign military deployment.

**Significance**: The House of Lords held that the disposition, engagement, and operational control of the armed forces are governed exclusively by the Crown’s prerogative powers. The court ruled that decisions regarding military deployments and national defense are non-justiciable matters solely within the discretion of the executive.

##### Entering international treaties without parliamentary approval
###### Case: R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union [2017] UKSC 5 (Miller I)

**Facts**: The Government sought to trigger Article 50 (to begin Brexit negotiations) using the royal prerogative in foreign affairs, arguing it could withdraw from treaties without Parliament.

**Significance**: The Supreme Court ruled 8-3 that the Government could not use prerogative power to nullify rights created by Parliament (via the European Communities Act 1972). The Court held that parliamentary approval was required, forcing the Government to pass the European Union (Notification of Withdrawal) Act 2017. This case reaffirmed parliamentary sovereignty over executive treaty-making powers.

###### Case: JH Rayner (Mincing Lane) Ltd v Department of Trade and Industry [1990] 2 AC 418 (International Tin Council Case)

**Facts**: Creditors sought to hold the UK government directly liable for contracts entered into by the International Tin Council, an international body created by an unratified treaty.

**Significance**: The House of Lords affirmed the dualist model of the UK constitution. Foreign treaty-making and entry into international agreements are prerogative powers exercised on the international plane; such treaties do not automatically alter domestic law or confer enforceable rights without express statutory implementation by Parliament.

##### Granting pardons without parliamentary scrutiny
###### Case: Derek Bentley (1953 conviction, 1998 royal pardon) / R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex parte Bentley [1994] QB 349

**Facts**: Bentley, a 19-year-old with learning difficulties, was hanged in 1953 for his role in a police officer’s murder, despite being under arrest at the time. His conviction was quashed by the Court of Appeal in 1998, following a 1993 Home Office decision granting a posthumous pardon in respect of the death sentence under the royal prerogative of mercy.

**Significance**: The case illustrates the unfettered executive power to grant pardons without parliamentary debate. While the Divisional Court held that exercise of the prerogative of mercy was justiciable and susceptible to judicial review (*ex p Bentley* [1994]), courts remain reluctant to challenge the substantive merits of executive pardons (*Bentley v UK* [1998] ECHR).

###### Case: R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex parte Shields [2008] EWHC 3112 (Admin)

**Facts**: A UK citizen convicted of an offense in Bulgaria requested a royal pardon under the prerogative of mercy after returning to serve his sentence in the UK, presenting evidence clearing him of the crime. The Home Secretary refused on policy grounds regarding foreign convictions.

**Significance**: The High Court confirmed that while decisions made under the royal prerogative of mercy are formally subject to judicial review, the court's scope of intervention is narrow. The case demonstrated the expansive discretion remaining with the executive in deciding when to grant or withhold clemency without parliamentary involvement.

##### Interference with common law and statutory rights without clear parliamentary mandates
###### Case: R (Miller) v The Prime Minister; Cherry v Advocate General for Scotland [2019] UKSC 41 (Miller II / Cherry)

**Facts**: In September 2019, the Prime Minister advised the Queen to prorogue Parliament for five weeks during critical negotiations preceding the UK’s exit from the EU, effectively preventing Parliament from sitting or exercising oversight.

**Significance**: The Supreme Court unanimously held that the Prime Minister's advice to prorogue Parliament was unlawful, null, and of no effect. The Court affirmed that executive prerogative powers cannot be exercised in a manner that prevents Parliament from performing its constitutional functions without reasonable justification, reinforcing parliamentary accountability over executive action.

###### Case: R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex parte Fire Brigades Union [1995] 2 AC 513

**Facts**: Parliament enacted provisions in the Criminal Justice Act 1988 for a statutory compensation scheme, leaving its commencement date to the Home Secretary. The Home Secretary announced he would not bring those provisions into force and instead used prerogative powers to introduce an alternative, less generous tariff-based scheme.

**Significance**: The House of Lords held that the executive could not use royal prerogative powers to enact a scheme that directly conflicted with or frustrated an Act of Parliament, even if that statutory provision had not yet formally been brought into effect.

##### Excluding governmental decisions from judicial review through ouster clauses
###### Case: R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22

**Facts**: Section 67(8) of the Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 stated that determinations of the Investigatory Powers Tribunal "shall not be questioned in any court." The government argued this ouster clause barred judicial review of the tribunal's decisions by the High Court.

**Significance**: The Supreme Court ruled 4-3 that despite explicit statutory language, the ouster clause did not exclude the High Court's jurisdiction to review decisions infected by errors of law. The judgment established a strong constitutional presumption that Parliament does not intend to create "law-free zones" or shield executive/tribunal decisions from judicial oversight, safeguarding the rule of law.

###### Case: Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission [1969] 2 AC 147

**Facts**: Section 4(4) of the Foreign Compensation Act 1950 provided that the Commission's determinations "shall not be called into question in any court of law." The Commission misconstrued the statutory framework when rejecting a compensation claim.

**Significance**: The House of Lords held that an administrative decision affected by a jurisdictional error of law is a nullity and not a valid "determination" at all. As a result, statutory ouster clauses cannot shield legally flawed executive or administrative decisions from judicial review.

###### Statutory Development: Section 2, Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 (Cart Ouster Clause)

**Facts**: Parliament enacted legislation explicitly seeking to remove the High Court’s judicial review jurisdiction over decisions of the Upper Tribunal refusing permission to appeal (reversing the landmark precedent in *R (Cart) v Upper Tribunal* [2011] UKSC 28).

**Significance**: This statutory intervention represents a direct attempt by the executive and legislature to limit judicial review mechanism in immigration and tribunal appeals, re-igniting debates over statutory ouster clauses and judicial independence.

#### Other misuse of Executive Authority

##### Dissolving Parliament at politically advantageous times & manipulating election timing
###### Statutory Example: Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 (Repeal of Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011)

**Facts**: The Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 (FTPA) removed the Prime Minister’s unilateral power to call general elections by requiring a two-thirds Commons majority or a vote of no confidence. In 2022, the government passed the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act, which repealed the FTPA and restored the royal prerogative power to dissolve Parliament on the advice of the Prime Minister.

**Significance**: Restoring the prerogative gave the executive the ability to schedule general elections at times advantageous to the governing party. Additionally, Section 3 of the 2022 Act contains an explicit statutory ouster clause asserting that courts cannot review decisions relating to the exercise of dissolution powers.

##### Appointments to key public offices without oversight
###### Executive Mechanism: Direct Appointments under Section 3.6 of the Governance Code on Public Appointments

**Facts**: Public appointments are intended to be regulated by the *Governance Code on Public Appointments*, overseen by the Commissioner for Public Appointments. However, Section 3.6 of the Code permits Ministers to make appointments "without a competition" under exceptional circumstances. Departments have routinely used this clause to make direct ministerial appointments (e.g., departmental non-executive directors, taskforce leads, and advisers) without competitive selection processes.

**Significance**: Utilizing direct appointments allows the executive to bypass independent merit-based procedures and select committee pre-appointment scrutiny, enabling ministers to place political allies in public roles and raising concerns regarding patronage and politicization.

###### Executive Mechanism: Civil Service Order in Council 1995 Amendments (1997 Prerogative Order)

**Facts**: In 1997, the executive amended the Civil Service Order in Council using royal prerogative powers to permit designated political advisers (such as the Prime Minister's Chief of Staff and Press Secretary) to give direct operational orders to permanent civil servants.

**Significance**: This demonstrated how prerogative instruments can be deployed to alter civil service governance and management structures without introducing primary legislation or undergoing prior parliamentary debate.

##### Lack of transparency in critical executive decisions
###### Case: R (Good Law Project) v Secretary of State for Health and Social Care [2021] EWHC 346 (Admin)

**Facts**: During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Health Secretary awarded public procurement contracts for Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) through a high-priority "VIP lane" reserved for candidates recommended by Ministers, MPs, and officials, without public tender or timely publication of contract notices.

**Significance**: The High Court ruled that the Health Secretary had acted unlawfully by failing to comply with the statutory duty of transparency under the Public Contracts Regulations, which required public notice of awarded contracts within 30 days. The case highlighted how executive action during emergency situations can compromise statutory transparency and competitive fairness requirements.

These cases reflect ongoing tensions between executive power and judicial oversight, with concerns about eroding checks and balances.

### 5. Psychological Operations

Psychological operations (PsyOps) are planned operations designed to convey selected information and indicators to foreign audiences to influence their emotions, attitudes, and behaviors. In the context of the UK, these operations have traditionally been used in military and intelligence contexts to shape the perceptions of adversaries or foreign populations. However, concerns have grown about their application in domestic settings.

Types of Psychological Operations

##### Strategic PsyOps
These are large-scale operations designed to influence the attitudes and behaviors of entire populations or significant segments thereof. They often involve coordinated campaigns across multiple media platforms, including social media, television, and print. Strategic PsyOps may aim to undermine enemy morale, promote government narratives, or discourage support for opposing forces. In the UK, strategic PsyOps have been alleged in the context of counter-terrorism efforts, where the government has sought to shape public perception of threats and responses to them.

##### Tactical PsyOps
Tactical PsyOps are smaller-scale operations focused on specific, immediate objectives. These might include influencing the behavior of a particular group or individual, such as discouraging enemy combatants from fighting or persuading a local population to cooperate with authorities. In domestic contexts, tactical PsyOps have been accused of targeting specific communities or activist groups to undermine their cohesion or discredit their messages.

##### Deception Operations
Deception PsyOps involve the deliberate spread of false or misleading information to achieve a strategic advantage. This can include the creation of fake personas, the fabrication of evidence, or the manipulation of existing narratives. The 77th Brigade has been implicated in such operations, with reports suggesting that it has used fake social media accounts to spread government-approved narratives and counter what it perceives as misinformation.

##### Propaganda and Misinformation Campaigns
These operations focus on shaping public opinion by controlling the flow of information. This can involve the suppression of dissenting voices, the amplification of favorable narratives, and the use of state-controlled media to influence public perception. In the UK, concerns have been raised about the government's role in shaping online discourse, particularly in the context of social media, where it has been accused of monitoring and manipulating public opinion.

##### Psychological Harassment (Zersetzung-like Tactics)
While not officially acknowledged, there have been allegations of the UK employing psychological harassment tactics similar to the Stasi's Zersetzung methods. These tactics involve systematic psychological manipulation, surveillance, and harassment of individuals or groups deemed to be threats. The goal is to destabilize, demoralize, or discredit targets through covert means, such as spreading rumors, manipulating personal relationships, or creating a climate of fear and uncertainty.

The deployment of psychological operations, whether in military or domestic contexts, raises significant ethical and legal questions. The lack of transparency surrounding these operations, combined with their potential for abuse, underscores the need for robust oversight and accountability mechanisms. As the boundaries between foreign and domestic operations blur, there is a growing risk that PsyOps could be used to suppress dissent, manipulate public opinion, and undermine democratic processes.


### 6. Zersetzung 
It's such an extensive system that it deserves a session of its own despite being a kind of Psychological Warfare.

Zersetzung, a Stasi tactic of psychological harassment, was a method used by the East German secret police to systematically destabilize and demoralize individuals perceived as threats to the state. The term translates to "decomposition" or "undermining," and it involved a range of covert tactics, including surveillance, psychological manipulation, and the spreading of disinformation to isolate and discredit targets.

While it is not officially documented in the UK, its principles—**systematic psychological manipulation, surveillance, and harassment**—have been alleged in modern contexts, particularly in discussions about **state surveillance and PsyOps**. The UK's *15 Psychological Operations Group* and *77th Brigade* have been linked to **domestic and foreign psychological operations**, raising concerns about ethical boundaries and transparency. Reports from whistleblowers and investigative journalists suggest that similar tactics may have been employed against activists, journalists, and political dissidents within the UK. These tactics allegedly include the use of fake social media accounts to harass and discredit individuals, the manipulation of personal and professional relationships, and the creation of a climate of fear and paranoia. The lack of official acknowledgment or oversight makes it difficult to verify these claims, but the parallels with historical Zersetzung tactics are striking and deeply concerning.

## Remedying
### UK Government Crackdown on Honour-Based Abuse
The UK government announced a crackdown on honour-based abuse, including forced marriage, female genital mutilation, and honour killings. Police will receive training to recognize signs of such abuse, and a new legal definition of honour-based abuse will be introduced. This follows criticism that these crimes have been allowed to happen in the shadows due to misunderstandings by professionals.

While this is a step toward addressing systemic abuse, it also highlights historical failures in protecting vulnerable groups and the need for greater accountability.
