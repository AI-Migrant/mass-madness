# Deep Dive into Moral Entrepreneurs with Case Studies

## Question

This report investigates the sociological concept of moral entrepreneurs, first articulated by Howard S. Becker in 1963, and examines its application through historical and contemporary case studies. The research seeks to understand how individuals, groups, and organizations act as moral entrepreneurs to shape social norms, create or enforce rules, and define deviance, thereby influencing law, public policy, and societal values.

## Executive Summary

1. Moral entrepreneurs are individuals, groups, or organizations that seek to influence social groups to adopt or maintain norms based on perceived boundaries of altruism, deviance, duty, or compassion. The concept was coined by Howard S. Becker in his 1963 work, Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance.

2. Becker distinguishes between two primary types of moral entrepreneurs: rule creators (crusading reformers who identify social evils and advocate for new rules) and rule enforcers (those who implement and uphold existing rules).

3. The term norm entrepreneur, coined by Cass Sunstein in 1996, extends this concept to the realm of social norm change, emphasizing the role of persuasion in the norm life cycle.

4. Historical case studies demonstrate the power of moral entrepreneurship in shaping major social policies, including the Temperance Movement and Prohibition in the United States, and Harry Anslinger's campaign against marijuana in the 1930s.

5. Contemporary examples, such as the #MeToo movement and climate activism led by figures like Greta Thunberg, illustrate the ongoing relevance of moral entrepreneurship in modern social change.

6. Moral entrepreneurs employ rhetorical strategies such as typification to define social problems, create moral panics, and mobilize public support for their causes.

7. The success of moral entrepreneurs depends on their access to power, public support, the ability to generate awareness, and the capacity to propose clear solutions to perceived problems.


## Methodology

This research employed a multi-stage investigation approach:

1. **Conceptual Foundation**: Began with an examination of Howard S. Becker's original work on moral entrepreneurs from Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance (1963), supplemented by secondary analyses and contemporary interpretations.

2. **Historical Case Study Analysis**: Investigated documented cases of moral entrepreneurship in historical contexts, focusing on the Temperance Movement, Prohibition, and the criminalization of marijuana in the United States.

3. **Contemporary Case Study Analysis**: Examined modern examples of moral entrepreneurship, including the #MeToo movement and climate activism, to understand the evolution and current applications of the concept.

4. **Source Triangulation**: Cross-referenced academic sources, government documents, news articles, and organizational materials to validate findings and ensure comprehensive coverage.

5. **Theoretical Framework**: Applied Becker's labeling theory and the concept of moral panic to analyze how moral entrepreneurs define deviance and shape social control.

Search angles included: sociological theory of deviance, historical social movements, drug policy history, contemporary social activism, public health campaigns, and the role of media in moral crusades.

Source types consulted: peer-reviewed academic articles, government reports, encyclopedia entries, news articles, and organizational websites.


## Findings

### Theoretical Foundations

#### Howard Becker's Original Concept

Howard S. Becker introduced the term moral entrepreneur in his seminal work, Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance (1963), to explore the relationship between law and morality and to explain how deviant social categories become defined and entrenched. Becker's labeling theory posits that deviance is not an inherent quality of an act but rather a consequence of how social groups react to and label behavior as deviant.

Becker identifies two categories of moral entrepreneurs:

1. **Rule Creators**: These are crusading reformers who believe that existing rules are inadequate because some social evil profoundly disturbs them. They operate with an absolute ethic, viewing the target behavior as truly and totally evil. The prototype is the crusading reformer who feels that nothing can be right in the world until rules are made to correct the perceived evil. Rule creators are typically individuals in positions of power who campaign to have certain deviant behaviors outlawed.

2. **Rule Enforcers**: These are individuals and organizations responsible for implementing and upholding the rules created by rule creators. They enforce the moral standards established through the rule-creating process.

Becker's approach views deviance as the creation of social groups, not the quality of some act or behavior. He argues that behavior defined as criminal is dynamic and changes throughout time based on which laws are enforced and which moral entrepreneurs are successful in their crusades.

#### Extension to Norm Entrepreneurship

Cass Sunstein's 1996 paper, Social Norms and Social Roles, introduced the related concept of norm entrepreneur. Sunstein argues that social conditions are often more fragile than typically assumed, as they depend on social norms to which many may not be strongly aligned. Norm entrepreneurs are individuals interested in changing social norms through persuasion, driving the norm life cycle from emergence to potential norm bandwagons and norm cascades.

Martha Finnemore and Kathryn Sikkink's work on international norm dynamics further developed this concept, identifying norm entrepreneurs as agents who seek to influence groups to adopt or maintain social norms based on assumed boundaries of altruism, deviance, duty, or compassion.

### Mechanisms of Moral Entrepreneurship

#### Typification and Social Problem Construction

Moral entrepreneurs play a critical role in the creation of social problems through the process of typification. Typification involves characterizing a problem's nature by suggesting it is best understood from a particular perspective (medical, moral, criminal, political, etc.). By identifying entire groups with particular behaviors and creating fear that these behaviors represent a danger to society, moral entrepreneurs aim to place issues on the public agenda and label certain actions as social problems.

This process is central to moral emergence and moral panic, as moral entrepreneurs call attention to or even create issues by using language that names, interprets, and dramatizes them. They often employ emotional appeals that bypass rational calculation and stir feelings of oneness with or separateness from the groups they seek to include or exclude from the moral community.

#### Power and Social Context

The degree of a moral entrepreneur's power is highly dependent upon the social and cultural context. Successful moral crusades are generally dominated by those in the upper social strata of society. Moral crusaders must have power, public support, generate public awareness of the issue, and be able to propose a clear and acceptable solution to the problem.

According to Richard Posner, moral entrepreneurs are people with the power to change our moral intuitions. They do not use rational argument but rather mix appeals to self-interest with emotional appeals that stir inarticulable feelings of oneness with or separateness from the people or causes they address.

The sociology of social control seeks to predict and explain the behavior of both rule creators and rule enforcers. Among the social conditions identified as sources of moralism are status superiority and social remoteness between the agents of social control and the people whose behavior they regulate. The most likely targets of moral entrepreneurs are those who are socially inferior, culturally different, and personally unknown, as their behavior is most likely to seem objectionable.

#### Moral Panic and Deviance Construction

Moral entrepreneurs are central to the construction of social deviance, including the development of drug scares and other moral panics. They assign responsibility to specific behaviors or substances for preexisting public problems, thereby expanding the power of social control.

Once moral entrepreneurs define the behaviors of particular individuals or groups as deviant or a moral threat, the entire group may be seen by society as a deviant subculture. This can lead to moral panics, where society rallies behind the moral entrepreneur's aims through the redefining of behaviors and groups as deviant or problematic.

### Historical Case Studies

#### Case Study 1: The Temperance Movement and Prohibition

**Overview**: The temperance movement in the United States sought to curb the consumption of alcohol and had a large influence on American politics and society in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, culminating in the prohibition of alcohol through the Eighteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution from 1920 to 1933.

**Moral Entrepreneurs**: The movement was led by various moral entrepreneurs, including:

- **Lyman Beecher**: A Presbyterian minister who co-founded the American Temperance Society and advocated for a ban on the manufacture and sale of liquor to foster individual morality and social order.
- **Women's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU)**: A prominent organization that campaigned for temperance and other social reforms.
- **National Prohibition Party**: Political actors who pushed for legislative solutions to the perceived alcohol problem.

**Strategies**: Temperance advocates employed several strategies characteristic of moral entrepreneurship:

1. **Typification**: They characterized alcohol consumption as a moral evil that led to crime, family strife, illness, immorality, poverty, and workplace injuries.
2. **Public Awareness Campaigns**: Used visual media like lithographs (e.g., The Drunkard's Progress) to dramatize the consequences of alcohol use.
3. **Legislative Advocacy**: Successfully pushed for local, state, and eventually federal legislation, culminating in the Eighteenth Amendment.
4. **Coalition Building**: Formed alliances with employers (including Andrew Carnegie) who sought to stem alcoholism's effects on productivity and workplace safety.

**Outcomes**: The movement achieved initial successes at the local and state levels before securing national legislation. Prohibition was most successful in rural southern and western states and less successful in more urban states. The experience of alcohol prohibition in the USA led to questioning the creation and enforcement of rules in general.

**Analysis**: The temperance movement exemplifies Becker's concept of rule creators. The moral entrepreneurs in this case were primarily religious groups, lawmaking bodies, and stakeholders who believed alcohol represented a threatening social evil. They operated with an absolute ethic, viewing alcohol consumption as truly and totally evil, and employed various means to persuade others of this view.

In 2009, Becker partly revoked his claim that the moral entrepreneurs in the war against opiate drugs, marijuana, and alcohol were by and large motivated by their Puritan views on the vice of seeking pleasure and loss of control. This revision suggests a more nuanced understanding of the motivations behind moral entrepreneurship.


#### Case Study 2: Harry Anslinger and the Criminalization of Marijuana

**Overview**: Harry J. Anslinger, the first commissioner of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics (FBN), played a pivotal role in the criminalization of marijuana in the United States through the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937.

**Moral Entrepreneur Profile**: Harry J. Anslinger (1892-1975) served as commissioner of the U.S. Treasury Department's Federal Bureau of Narcotics from 1930 to 1962, spanning the presidencies of Hoover, Roosevelt, Truman, Eisenhower, and Kennedy. He was the first architect of the war on drugs.

**Strategies and Tactics**:

1. **Problem Construction**: Anslinger characterized marijuana as causing psychosis, insanity, violent crime, and murder. He claimed that young people were slaves to this narcotic and that it was far worse than morphine or cocaine.

2. **Rhetorical Devices**: Employed typification by framing marijuana use as a moral threat to youth and society. He frequently undermined scientific studies that minimized the danger of marijuana by citing specific instances of alleged harm.

3. **Propaganda**: Maintained Gore Files, a collection of 200 violent crimes allegedly linked to marijuana use, which were referenced in anti-marijuana campaigns and films like Reefer Madness.

4. **Racial and Ethnic Scapegoating**: Anslinger's anti-cannabis articles often contained racist themes, including claims about Mexican workers and African Americans using marijuana.

5. **Legislative Advocacy**: Championed the passage of the federal Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, which effectively criminalized marijuana throughout the country. He lobbied Congress and garnered support from President Franklin D. Roosevelt.

**Institutional Context**: The Federal Bureau of Narcotics, established in 1930, was the primary agency pursuing crimes related to narcotics including cannabis, opium, cocaine, and their derivatives. Under Anslinger, the FBN lobbied for harsh penalties for drug usage and treated all drug use as a criminal act without differentiating between casual use and addiction.

**Outcomes**: The Marihuana Tax Act of 1937 was signed by Roosevelt and took effect on October 1, 1937. This legislation criminalized the possession or sale of marijuana and contributed to popular perceptions that the drug was highly dangerous, addictive, and caused great social harm. Anslinger's approach influenced American drug policy for decades.

**Analysis**: Anslinger represents a classic example of Becker's rule creator moral entrepreneur. As a government official with institutional power, he identified marijuana use as a social evil and successfully advocated for its criminalization. His campaign demonstrates how moral entrepreneurs can use their positions of authority, rhetorical skills, and strategic framing to shape public policy and social norms.

The case also illustrates how moral entrepreneurs can employ fear-based appeals and selective use of evidence to support their positions. Anslinger's disregard for scientific evidence that contradicted his views and his use of racial stereotypes demonstrate the potentially problematic aspects of moral entrepreneurship.

**Contemporary Relevance**: Anslinger's rhetoric and approaches have echoes in contemporary drug policy debates. Recent renewed rhetoric favoring criminal penalties for marijuana use has been noted to echo earlier moral outrage from Anslinger.


#### Case Study 3: Anti-Tobacco Campaigns

**Overview**: Public health campaigns against tobacco use represent a different form of moral entrepreneurship, where the moral entrepreneurs are public health officials, medical professionals, and advocacy organizations seeking to reduce the health and social harms associated with smoking.

**Moral Entrepreneurs**: Anti-tobacco moral entrepreneurs include:

- Public health agencies and officials
- Medical professionals and researchers
- Advocacy organizations such as the American Cancer Society, American Heart Association, and American Lung Association
- Government bodies implementing tobacco control policies

**Strategies**:

1. **Medical Framing**: Characterized tobacco use as a public health problem rather than purely a moral failing, using medical and scientific evidence to support their claims.
2. **Economic Arguments**: Highlighted the economic costs of tobacco-related illnesses and lost productivity.
3. **Legislative Advocacy**: Pushed for tobacco taxes, advertising restrictions, smoking bans in public spaces, and warning labels on cigarette packages.
4. **Public Education**: Conducted extensive public education campaigns to raise awareness of the health risks of tobacco use.

**Outcomes**: These campaigns have contributed to significant decreases in smoking rates in many countries, though they have also been criticized for potentially increasing stigma against smokers.

**Analysis**: The anti-tobacco movement demonstrates how moral entrepreneurship can be employed for public health goals. Unlike the temperance movement or Anslinger's anti-marijuana campaign, which were often driven by moral or religious convictions, anti-tobacco efforts have been primarily framed in terms of health and economic benefits. This represents a shift in the moral entrepreneurship landscape, where scientific evidence and public health concerns can drive social change.

The movement also illustrates the tension between promoting public health and respecting individual autonomy, as some critics argue that aggressive anti-tobacco campaigns may stigmatize smokers rather than simply addressing the behavior.


### Contemporary Case Studies

#### Case Study 4: The #MeToo Movement

**Overview**: The #MeToo movement is a social movement against sexual harassment and assault that gained global prominence in 2017, though its origins trace back to activist Tarana Burke's work in 2006.

**Moral Entrepreneur Profile**: Tarana Burke, an American activist, began using the phrase Me Too in 2006 to raise awareness of the pervasiveness of sexual assault and harassment, particularly among women of color. The movement gained widespread attention in October 2017 when actress Alyssa Milano encouraged women to tweet about their experiences using the hashtag #MeToo.

**Strategies and Tactics**:

1. **Awareness Raising**: The movement used social media to create a viral campaign that demonstrated the widespread nature of sexual harassment and assault.
2. **Typification**: Framed sexual harassment and assault as systemic problems rather than isolated incidents, characterizing them as moral violations that require societal address.
3. **Collective Action**: Encouraged survivors to share their stories, creating a sense of solidarity and demonstrating the scale of the problem.
4. **Institutional Accountability**: Called for accountability from individuals, organizations, and institutions that had enabled or ignored harassment and assault.

**Outcomes**: The #MeToo movement has led to significant cultural shifts, including:

- Increased public awareness of sexual harassment and assault
- The downfall of numerous powerful individuals accused of misconduct
- Changes in workplace policies and procedures
- Ongoing debates about due process, power dynamics, and systemic change
- International spread of the movement, with variations in different countries

**Analysis**: The #MeToo movement represents a contemporary form of moral entrepreneurship where the moral entrepreneurs are primarily survivors and activists rather than institutional authorities. Tarana Burke and other activists have acted as rule creators, seeking to establish new norms around sexual behavior, consent, and accountability.

The movement demonstrates how moral entrepreneurship can emerge from grassroots activism and leverage social media to rapidly gain public support. It also illustrates the challenges of moral entrepreneurship in the digital age, where movements can quickly gain momentum but also face backlash and criticism.

The #MeToo movement has been described as a case study in how moral entrepreneurs can use personal narratives and collective action to redefine social norms and demand institutional change. It shows the power of moral entrepreneurship to address long-standing social problems and hold powerful individuals and institutions accountable.


#### Case Study 5: Climate Activism and Greta Thunberg

**Overview**: Youth-led climate activism, exemplified by Greta Thunberg's Fridays for Future movement, represents a contemporary form of moral entrepreneurship focused on addressing climate change.

**Moral Entrepreneur Profile**: Greta Thunberg, a Swedish environmental activist, began her school strike for climate action in August 2018 at age 15. Her solo protest outside the Swedish parliament inspired a global movement of student strikes demanding action on climate change.

**Strategies and Tactics**:

1. **Moral Framing**: Characterized climate change as a moral crisis requiring urgent action, framing it as an issue of intergenerational justice and responsibility.
2. **Typification**: Presented climate change as a threat to the future of humanity and the planet, requiring immediate and systemic responses.
3. **Youth Mobilization**: Leveraged the moral authority of youth, who have the most at stake in the long-term consequences of climate change.
4. **Global Coordination**: Used social media and digital communication to coordinate international strikes and build a global movement.
5. **Institutional Pressure**: Directed demands at governments, corporations, and international organizations to take concrete action on climate change.

**Outcomes**: The movement has achieved significant impact, including:

- Millions of students participating in climate strikes worldwide
- Increased media attention and public awareness of climate change
- Political responses from governments and international bodies
- Shifts in public discourse around climate change as a moral and ethical issue

**Analysis**: Greta Thunberg and the Fridays for Future movement demonstrate how moral entrepreneurship can operate in the context of global challenges and digital communication. Thunberg acts as a norm entrepreneur, seeking to change social norms around climate action and responsibility.

Academic analysis has described Thunberg as an international norm entrepreneur, using her moral authority and communication skills to influence global discourse and policy on climate change. Her ability to frame climate change as a moral issue and mobilize youth across the world illustrates the power of moral entrepreneurship in the contemporary era.

The case also highlights how moral entrepreneurs can emerge from unexpected sources (in this case, a teenager) and leverage digital platforms to amplify their message and build global movements. It demonstrates the ongoing relevance of Becker's concepts in understanding contemporary social change.


### Comparative Analysis

The case studies presented demonstrate both continuity and change in the phenomenon of moral entrepreneurship across different historical periods and social contexts.

**Continuities**:

1. **Moral Framing**: All cases involve the characterization of certain behaviors or conditions as moral problems requiring societal address.
2. **Typification**: Each case employs typification to define the nature of the problem and propose solutions.
3. **Power Dynamics**: The success of moral entrepreneurs depends on their access to power, whether institutional (Anslinger), social (temperance movement), or moral (Thunberg, Burke).
4. **Social Control**: Each case involves efforts to establish or enforce social norms and control deviant behavior.

**Changes**:

1. **Source of Authority**: Historical cases often relied on institutional or religious authority, while contemporary cases may draw on personal experience (MeToo) or moral authority (climate activism).
2. **Communication Methods**: Contemporary moral entrepreneurs leverage digital platforms and social media to rapidly gain support and mobilize action.
3. **Global Scope**: Contemporary moral entrepreneurship is more likely to have a global reach and address global issues.
4. **Scientific Basis**: Modern moral entrepreneurship, particularly in public health, is more likely to be grounded in scientific evidence.

**Effectiveness Factors**:

The case studies suggest that effective moral entrepreneurship requires:

1. Clear identification of a social problem
2. Emotional resonance with the target audience
3. Access to power and resources
4. Ability to generate public awareness
5. Proposal of clear, acceptable solutions
6. Adaptability to changing social contexts

## Open Questions

1. **Motivational Complexity**: To what extent are moral entrepreneurs driven by genuine altruism versus self-interest, institutional preservation, or other motivations? Becker's later revision of his views on Puritan motivations suggests this remains an open question.

2. **Effectiveness Measurement**: How can the effectiveness of moral entrepreneurship be measured beyond immediate policy outcomes? What are the long-term social impacts of successful moral crusades?

3. **Digital Age Dynamics**: How has the rise of social media and digital communication changed the dynamics of moral entrepreneurship? Do these platforms democratize moral entrepreneurship or simply amplify existing power structures?

4. **Backlash and Resistance**: What factors contribute to successful resistance against moral entrepreneurship? How do counter-movements emerge and gain traction?

5. **Global Moral Entrepreneurship**: How does moral entrepreneurship operate in international contexts, where cultural norms and legal systems vary significantly?

6. **Ethical Boundaries**: Where should the boundaries be drawn between legitimate moral entrepreneurship and moral coercion? How can societies balance moral progress with individual autonomy?

7. **Institutionalization**: How do the norms established by moral entrepreneurs become institutionalized, and what are the mechanisms for their persistence or change over time?


## Recommendations and Next Steps

### For Researchers

1. Conduct comparative studies of moral entrepreneurship across different cultural and historical contexts to identify universal patterns and context-specific variations.

2. Investigate the role of digital platforms in contemporary moral entrepreneurship, including the mechanisms by which movements gain traction and the challenges they face.

3. Examine the long-term consequences of successful moral crusades, including both intended and unintended outcomes.

4. Develop frameworks for evaluating the ethical dimensions of moral entrepreneurship, including the balance between social progress and individual rights.

5. Explore the intersection of moral entrepreneurship with other forms of social change, such as technological innovation, economic shifts, and demographic changes.


### For Practitioners and Policymakers

1. **Critical Evaluation**: When engaging with moral entrepreneurial efforts, critically evaluate the evidence and motivations behind them, recognizing that moral entrepreneurs may employ selective framing and emotional appeals.

2. **Inclusive Dialogue**: Ensure that policy responses to moral entrepreneurship include diverse perspectives and consider both intended and potential unintended consequences.

3. **Evidence-Based Approaches**: Ground moral entrepreneurial efforts in rigorous evidence and scientific research, particularly in areas like public health where objective data is available.

4. **Procedural Fairness**: In addressing the concerns raised by moral entrepreneurs, maintain commitment to due process, fairness, and the protection of individual rights.

5. **Adaptability**: Recognize that social norms and moral standards evolve over time, and maintain flexibility in responding to moral entrepreneurial efforts.
