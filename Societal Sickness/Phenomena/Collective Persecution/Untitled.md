# Participatory Persecution: Integrated Report (Historical Foundations + 2024–2026 Case Studies)
*A Comprehensive Analytical Report on Mechanisms, Dynamics, Mechanisms, Historical Trajectories, Digital Transformations, and Contemporary Case Studies and Evolutions*  

## Executive Summary

Participatory persecution refers to persecution in which **ordinary civilians become active agents** of harm — whether through denunciation, mob violence, digital harassment, or collaboration with state surveillance systems. Unlike purely top‑down repression, participatory persecution emerges when **states, institutions, or social movements mobilize horizontal violence**, encouraging neighbors, coworkers, and community members to participate.

This report integrates historical theory with recent case studies (2024–2026), showing how participatory persecution evolves across three major domains:

### 1. **Digital Participatory Persecution**
Platforms amplify harassment, doxxing, deepfakes, swatting, and coordinated mob attacks.  
Key cases include:
- UN Women global study (2025): 2/3 of activists experienced online violence; 41% suffered offline harm linked to digital attacks.  
- Burkina Faso’s state‑aligned digital militia (2026).  
- Singapore’s Online Safety Bill (2025).  
- German deepfake sexualized violence case (2026).  
- Romanian swatting spree targeting US officials (2026).

### 2. **Physical Participatory Persecution**
Mob violence resurged in multiple regions:
- Bangladesh (2024–2026): 153 mob‑violence deaths; lynching of a spiritual leader and Hindu garment worker.  
- Sri Lanka (2022–2026): lynching of a lawmaker by a 5,000‑person mob.  
- Nigeria (2026): vigilante massacres of Fulani herders; ~1,000 deaths in two months.

### 3. **State‑Sponsored Participatory Systems**
States formalize citizen participation:
- North Korea: informer networks, collective punishment, persecution of Christians, shoot‑on‑sight border orders.  
- China: social credit blacklisting, mass surveillance, neighbor reporting, 200M+ cameras.

### Key Findings
1. **Horizontal mobilization is increasingly algorithmic.**  
Digital mobs form faster, scale larger, and transition to offline harm more frequently.

2. **Material incentives remain central.**  
From historical Aryanization to modern land disputes in Nigeria, economic gain drives participation.

3. **Denunciation networks are expanding.**  
States increasingly rely on citizen reporting systems, both analog and digital.

4. **Collective punishment amplifies participation.**  
Targeting families or entire communities creates multiplier effects.

5. **Digital‑physical convergence is the most dangerous trend.**  
Online harassment now routinely escalates into offline violence.

### Top Recommendations (Condensed)
1. **Governments:** criminalize malicious denunciation; regulate deepfakes; create early‑warning systems for mob formation.  
2. **Platforms:** enforce anti‑doxxing rules; detect coordinated harassment; deploy rapid‑response teams in conflict zones.  
3. **Researchers:** develop standardized indicators for participatory persecution; build digital‑physical escalation models.  
4. **Civil society:** strengthen digital literacy; create victim‑support networks; deploy counter‑narratives.  
5. **International bodies:** monitor state‑aligned digital militias; classify algorithmic persecution as a human‑rights risk.

## Introduction

Participatory persecution is a form of systemic oppression in which **civilian populations actively participate** in harming targeted groups. It differs from conventional state repression by relying on **horizontal violence** — neighbors attacking neighbors — often under state encouragement or impunity.

This report merges historical theory with contemporary case studies to answer six core research questions:

1. **What mechanisms transform ordinary civilians into perpetrators?**  
2. **How do states mobilize or incentivize citizen participation?**  
3. **How does digital persecution escalate into physical violence?**  
4. **What patterns emerge across recent (2024–2026) global cases?**  
5. **How can participatory persecution be measured and monitored?**  
6. **What interventions can reduce or prevent participatory persecution?**

### Scope
This report covers:
- Historical cases (Nazi Germany, Rwanda, Cultural Revolution)  
- Digital cases (deepfakes, doxxing, swatting, online militias)  
- Physical mob violence (Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nigeria)  
- State‑sponsored systems (North Korea, China)  

### Working Definition
Participatory persecution is **state‑sanctioned or socially legitimized persecution in which civilians actively participate**, through:
- Direct violence  
- Denunciation  
- Digital harassment  
- Material exploitation  
- Bureaucratic facilitation  
- Collective punishment  
- Surveillance and reporting  

### Why This Concept Matters Now
The rise of algorithmic amplification, deepfake technology, and state‑aligned digital militias has created a new frontier where **digital persecution rapidly becomes physical persecution**.

This report provides a unified framework for understanding these dynamics.

## Theoretical Framework

Participatory persecution emerges from the interaction of **macro‑level state structures** and **micro‑level civilian actions**, mediated by a **mobilization interface**.

```
[ Macro-Level State Apparatus ]
(Ideology, Legal Frameworks, Propaganda)
│
▼
[ Mobilization Interface ]
(Incentives, Denunciation Systems, Impunity)
│
▼
[ Micro-Level Social Action ]
(Civilian Perpetration, Denunciation, Theft)
```


### Key Characteristics
1. **Horizontal Violence**  
Persecution occurs between civilians rather than solely through state agents.

2. **State Sanction & Impunity**  
Legal frameworks or propaganda lower moral/legal barriers.

3. **Material Incentives**  
Perpetrators often gain property, land, jobs, or social status.

4. **Denunciation Networks**  
Citizens voluntarily report neighbors, coworkers, or dissidents.

## Theoretical Framework (Expanded)  
### Socio‑Psychological Drivers of Participatory Persecution

Participatory persecution does not emerge spontaneously. It requires a combination of **psychological**, **social**, and **structural** forces that reshape how individuals perceive themselves, their neighbors, and the legitimacy of violence.

### 1. In‑Group / Out‑Group Polarization  
Perpetrators come to see the targeted group as fundamentally different, dangerous, or morally inferior.  
Mechanisms include:
- Propaganda framing minorities as existential threats  
- Historical grievances reframed as collective danger  
- Social media echo chambers reinforcing identity boundaries  

### 2. Diffusion of Responsibility  
When “everyone is doing it,” individual guilt diminishes.  
People feel:
- Their actions are part of a collective movement  
- Responsibility lies with leaders or the crowd  
- Violence is justified because others participate  

### 3. Dehumanization  
Victims are described as pests, parasites, traitors, or criminals.  
This removes moral barriers and enables:
- Cruelty  
- Torture  
- Public humiliation  
- Collective punishment  

### 4. Social Norming & Conformity  
Individuals fear:
- Being ostracized  
- Being labeled sympathizers  
- Becoming targets themselves  

Thus, participation becomes a **social expectation**.

### 5. Opportunism  
Material incentives — land, property, jobs, debt elimination — often motivate participation.  
Historical and modern examples show:
- Aryanization of Jewish property  
- Land seizures in Nigeria  
- Professional advancement during China’s Cultural Revolution

## Structural Mechanics & Dynamics

Participatory persecution relies on **mechanisms that transform ordinary civilians into perpetrators**. These mechanisms operate across legal, ideological, economic, and informational domains.

### Table: Structural Mechanisms of Participatory Persecution

| **Mechanism** | **Description** | **Enabling Conditions** | **Observable Indicators** |
|---------------|------------------|--------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Legal De‑individualization** | Removal of legal protections from targeted groups | Emergency laws, racial codes, blacklists | Sudden legal status changes, mass detentions |
| **Ideological Alignment** | Violence framed as patriotic or necessary | Propaganda, education, state media | Hate speech spikes, ideological slogans |
| **Material Redistribution** | Transfer of assets to compliant citizens | Economic desperation, corruption | Property seizures, forced sales |
| **Informational Mobilization** | Citizens encouraged to report others | Surveillance systems, informer networks | Increase in denunciations, arrests |
| **Algorithmic Amplification** | Platforms accelerate outrage and mob formation | Engagement‑driven algorithms | Viral harassment, coordinated doxxing |
| **Collective Punishment** | Families or communities punished together | Authoritarian legal frameworks | Multi‑family detentions, village‑level reprisals |

## Spectrum of Civilian Involvement

Civilian participation exists along a continuum:

1. **Direct Violence**  
Pogroms, lynchings, vigilante killings, mob attacks.

2. **Denunciation**  
Reporting neighbors, coworkers, or dissidents.

3. **Material Exploitation**  
Taking over expropriated homes, businesses, or land.

4. **Bureaucratic Facilitation**  
Teachers, doctors, clerks enforcing discriminatory policies.

5. **Passive Compliance & Voyeurism**  
Watching public humiliations, sharing harmful content online.

## Historical Case Studies

### 1. Nazi Germany and Occupied Europe (1933–1945)

- Mechanisms
  - Public denunciation  
  - Aryanization of Jewish property  
  - Local participation in pogroms (e.g., Jedwabne, 1941)

- Civilian Roles
  - Reporting neighbors hiding Jews  
  - Participating in pogroms  
  - Acquiring Jewish businesses and household goods  

- Lessons
  - Material incentives can mobilize large civilian participation  
  - Propaganda reframes violence as patriotic duty  
  - Denunciation networks become self‑sustaining

  - Community networks repurposed for violence

  - Local militia (Interahamwe)

-  Civilian Roles
  - Villagers killing neighbors with agricultural tools  
  - Community pressure forcing participation  

-  Lessons
  - Decentralized violence can be extremely rapid  
  - Propaganda can override long‑standing social ties

### 3. China’s Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)

- Mechanisms
  - Red Guard mobilization  
  - Struggle sessions  
  - Big‑character posters

- Civilian Roles
  - Students attacking teachers  
  - Youth denouncing parents  
  - Public humiliation rituals  

- Lessons
  - Ideological fervor can collapse traditional hierarchies  
  - Youth mobilization is a powerful vector for persecution

**High** — UN Women, European Commission, and partner organizations.

# Digital‑Physical Escalation  
## How Online Participatory Persecution Translates into Offline Harm

Digital persecution is no longer confined to screens. Across multiple regions, online harassment, doxxing, deepfakes, and coordinated mob attacks have escalated into **physical violence**, **state action**, and **collective punishment**.

This section explains **how** and **why** digital persecution becomes physical persecution.

---

# 1. Escalation Pathways

Digital‑to‑physical escalation typically follows **three major pathways**:

---

## Pathway A — Algorithmic Amplification → Mass Outrage → Offline Mob Formation

### Mechanism
1. A triggering event (video, rumor, accusation) goes viral.  
2. Algorithms amplify outrage‑laden content.  
3. Digital mobs form, spreading calls for punishment.  
4. Offline mobs assemble rapidly.

### Example: Bangladesh (2026)
- A resurfaced video allegedly insulting the Quran went viral.  
- Outrage spread across Facebook and WhatsApp.  
- A **200‑person mob** formed within hours.  
- The spiritual leader was beaten to death.

### Key Insight
Digital outrage compresses the time between **trigger** and **violence**.

---

## Pathway B — Doxxing → Target Localization → Physical Attacks

### Mechanism
1. Personal information is leaked online.  
2. Civilians share addresses, workplaces, family details.  
3. Offline actors use this information to locate targets.  
4. Physical harassment, assault, or mob violence follows.

### Example: UN Women Study (2025)
- 41% of online harassment victims experienced offline harm.  
- Doxxing was a major predictor of physical stalking and assault.

### Key Insight
Doxxing transforms digital hostility into actionable physical targeting.

---

## Pathway C — Deepfakes → Reputation Destruction → Offline Retaliation

### Mechanism
1. Deepfake sexualized or incriminating content circulates.  
2. Civilians believe the content is real.  
3. Offline threats, harassment, or violence escalate.  
4. Victims face social ostracization or physical danger.

### Example: Germany (2026)
- Deepfake pornography triggered death threats.  
- Offline harassment followed public disclosure.

### Key Insight
Deepfakes accelerate reputational harm and trigger offline retaliation.

# 1. Recommendations for Governments

Governments play a central role in shaping the legal and institutional environment that either enables or constrains participatory persecution.

## 1.1 Criminalize Malicious Denunciation  
**Implementation Pathway**  
- Introduce laws penalizing false or malicious reporting.  
- Establish independent review bodies to evaluate denunciations.  
- Protect whistleblowers who expose misuse of reporting systems.

**Success Metrics**  
- Reduction in false denunciation cases.  
- Increase in successful prosecutions of malicious actors.

**Challenges**  
- Balancing free expression with anti‑abuse measures.  
- Preventing misuse of laws by authoritarian regimes.

---

## 1.2 Regulate Deepfakes and Synthetic Media  
**Implementation Pathway**  
- Require provenance metadata for AI‑generated media.  
- Criminalize non‑consensual sexualized deepfakes.  
- Mandate rapid takedown procedures for harmful synthetic content.

**Success Metrics**  
- Number of deepfake takedowns.  
- Reduction in deepfake‑related harassment cases.

**Challenges**  
- Cross‑border enforcement.  
- Rapid evolution of generative AI tools.

---

## 1.3 Establish Early‑Warning Systems for Mob Formation  
**Implementation Pathway**  
- Monitor hate speech spikes and viral inflammatory content.  
- Coordinate with platforms for real‑time alerts.  
- Deploy rapid‑response community mediation teams.

**Success Metrics**  
- Number of prevented mob incidents.  
- Time between digital trigger and intervention.

**Challenges**  
- Resource constraints in rural areas.  
- Risk of over‑surveillance.

---

## 1.4 Strengthen Protections for Journalists and Activists  
**Implementation Pathway**  
- Provide emergency relocation support.  
- Create secure reporting channels for harassment.  
- Enforce strict penalties for coordinated digital attacks.

**Success Metrics**  
- Reduction in attacks on journalists.  
- Increased reporting of harassment incidents.

**Challenges**  
- Political resistance in fragile democracies.  
- Limited capacity in conflict zones.

# Annex A — Glossary of Key Terms

**Participatory Persecution**  
Persecution in which civilians actively participate through violence, denunciation, digital harassment, or collaboration with state systems.

**Horizontal Violence**  
Violence between civilians rather than state agents.

**Denunciation Networks**  
Systems encouraging citizens to report neighbors or coworkers.

**Algorithmic Amplification**  
Platform algorithms that boost outrage‑laden content, accelerating mob formation.

**Collective Punishment**  
Punishing families or communities for the actions of individuals.

**Digital‑Physical Escalation**  
The process by which online harassment leads to offline violence.
