# Participatory Persecution: Recent Case Studies and Analysis

## Question

This report investigates the concept of participatory persecution, defined as the active involvement of community members, citizens, or the general population in the persecution of individuals or groups, either independently or in collaboration with state actors. The research focuses on identifying recent case studies from 2024-2026, analyzing patterns across digital, physical, and state-sponsored contexts, and assessing the mechanisms through which participation is mobilized.

## Executive Summary

Participatory persecution represents a significant evolution in the landscape of human rights violations, characterized by the decentralization of persecution through citizen involvement. Recent evidence from 2024-2026 demonstrates four primary manifestations: digital mob harassment, physical mob violence, state-sponsored citizen reporting systems, and collective punishment regimes.

Key findings include the weaponization of social media platforms for coordinated harassment campaigns, the resurgence of mob violence in multiple regions, the institutionalization of citizen participation in surveillance states, and the systematic use of collective punishment to suppress dissent. The convergence of digital and physical participatory persecution, particularly in contexts where state actors provide implicit or explicit support, represents the most dangerous trend.

The most severe cases involve state-sponsored systems that formalize citizen participation in persecution, particularly in North Korea and China, where legal frameworks and social credit systems create structured mechanisms for mass participation in monitoring, reporting, and punishing targeted individuals and groups.

## Methodology

This research employed iterative web and news searches to identify recent case studies of participatory persecution. Search angles included:

- Direct queries for the term "participatory persecution" across academic, legal, and human rights databases
- Related concept searches for digital mob harassment, doxxing, swatting, and online violence
- Physical participatory persecution through mob violence, lynching, and vigilante justice
- State-sponsored participatory systems including social credit, neighbor reporting, and informer networks
- Collective punishment and guilt-by-association mechanisms

Source types prioritized recent news reports (2024-2026), human rights organization publications, academic analyses, and official government documentation. The investigation cross-checked claims across multiple sources and distinguished between verified facts, reported allegations, and analytical interpretations.

Search limitations included restricted access to some primary sources behind paywalls and the absence of a universally accepted definition of "participatory persecution" in existing literature, necessitating conceptual synthesis from related frameworks.

## Findings

### Digital Participatory Persecution

The digital sphere has emerged as the most dynamic frontier for participatory persecution, with social media platforms enabling rapid mobilization of large groups for coordinated harassment campaigns.

**UN Women Global Study (December 2025):** A comprehensive study by UN Women, conducted with partners including the European Commission and TheNerve, surveyed 6,900 human rights defenders, journalists, and activists across 119 countries. The report found that more than two-thirds experienced online violence, with 41% reporting offline attacks, abuse, or harassment directly linked to digital violence. These offline harms included physical or sexual assault, stalking, verbal harassment, and swatting—a tactic involving bogus emergency calls to provoke armed police responses. The study documented a doubling of real-world harm cases linked to online violence over the past five years, with 42% of respondents in 2025 identifying this "dangerous and potentially deadly trajectory." Women writers, influencers, and social media content providers focusing on human rights were identified as the most affected groups. The report also highlighted the role of artificial intelligence in generating deepfake images and manipulated content to amplify harassment campaigns.

**Singapore Online Safety Legislation (October 2025):** In response to rising online abuse, Singapore proposed the Online Safety (Relief and Accountability) Bill, establishing an Online Safety Commission with powers to compel social media firms to remove harmful content. The legislation addresses five initial categories of online harms: online harassment, doxxing (sharing personal information without permission), stalking, image-based child abuse, and sharing of private photos without permission. A government survey found that 84% of Singaporeans encountered harmful online content, and 33% experienced harmful online behavior in the past year. The bill provides legal mechanisms for victims to hold perpetrators accountable and requires online platforms to provide identities of anonymous posters when directed.

**Burkina Faso State-Sponsored Digital Militia (July 2026):** Reporters Without Borders documented the existence of a state-sponsored "online militia" in Burkina Faso, known as the Rapid Communication Intervention Battalion (BIR-C). This group, established by the ruling junta under Captain Ibrahim Traore, combines propaganda and disinformation with cyber-harassment against journalists and critics. The militia's activities include online harassment, manipulated images, insults, threats, smear campaigns, and disinformation. Analysis of a WhatsApp group associated with the militia revealed the involvement of high-level officials, including Defence Minister Celestin Simpore. The harassment campaigns specifically target journalists, with a notable case involving Sky News reporter Yousra Elbagir, who faced hundreds of messages seeking to discredit her after producing a report critical of the government.

**Romanian Swatting Case (April 2026):** Thomasz Szabo, a 27-year-old Romanian national, was sentenced to four years in prison for organizing a swatting spree targeting dozens of US government officials, including members of Congress, cabinet-level officials, federal judges, and agency heads. The campaign involved making hoax threats to provoke emergency police responses at targets' homes. Szabo's associates, including Nemanja Radovanovic of Serbia and Alan Filion (sentenced in February 2025), participated in making approximately 375 swatting calls between August 2022 and January 2024. Prosecutors noted that these activities offered "entertainment value" to the defendant and followers while causing significant real-world harm and diverting emergency resources from genuine crises.

**German Deepfake Case (April 2026):** Actress Collien Fernandes publicly accused her ex-husband of spreading fake pornographic images of her online, sparking a national debate in Germany about sexualized online violence. The case, described as the "digital Pelicot case" in reference to a French rape survivor, involved doctored deepfake videos and written content depicting Fernandes in degrading situations. Following her public disclosure, Fernandes received death threats alongside an outpouring of support. The case has pressured the German government to propose harsher penalties for online violence involving deepfakes.

### Physical Participatory Persecution: Mob Violence

Mob violence represents a traditional form of participatory persecution that has seen resurgence in multiple regions, often fueled by religious tensions, political instability, and social media amplification.

**Bangladesh Mob Killings (2024-2026):** Muslim-majority Bangladesh has experienced a sharp rise in religious intolerance and mob violence since the 2024 uprising that toppled Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina. In April 2026, a self-proclaimed spiritual leader, Shamim Reza Jahangir, was beaten to death by a mob of more than 200 people in Kushtia district after an old video resurfaced online showing him allegedly making derogatory remarks about the Quran. Police attempted to intervene but were overwhelmed by the crowd. According to the Dhaka-based rights organization Odhikar, at least 153 people were killed by mob violence from August 2024 to September 2025. In December 2025, a Hindu garment worker was lynched by a mob, further straining communal relations.

**Sri Lanka Lawmaker Lynching (February 2026):** A Sri Lankan court sentenced 12 men to death for the 2022 mob killing of lawmaker Amarakeerthi Athukorala during protests that toppled the president. Athukorala, aged 57, was lynched after opening fire on protesters blocking his car in Nittambuwa. He was overwhelmed by a mob of approximately 5,000 people and attempted to take refuge in a building before being killed. His bodyguard was also lynched in the same incident. The killing occurred amid nationwide protests against severe shortages of food, fuel, and medicines.

**Nigeria Vigilante Violence (2026):** Vigilante groups in north-central Nigeria's Niger state launched raids against ethnic Fulani herders, allegedly killing hundreds and accusing them of complicity in jihadist violence. The Kambari and Bargawa ethnic groups, primarily farmers, have been sacking Fulani herding settlements in Agwara district, killing herders, looting livestock, and destroying homes. Armed vigilantes intensified attacks in August 2026, "riding into communities on motorcycles, shooting and hacking anyone on sight." The local Fulani chief reported approximately 1,000 deaths over the previous two months. In March 2026, gunmen opened fire in a Jos university neighborhood bar, sparking mob retaliation that killed nearly three dozen people in total.

**Historical Context - Mary Turner Lynching Memorial (December 2025):** A historical marker commemorating the 1918 lynching of Mary Turner, an eight-months-pregnant woman killed by a white mob in Georgia for denouncing her husband's lynching, went on display in Atlanta. The marker, repeatedly vandalized in recent years, serves as a reminder of America's history of racial terror lynchings. Between 1880-1930, as many as 550 people were killed in Georgia through mob violence, with no charges ever brought against known participants. The Equal Justice Initiative has documented over 4,400 racial terror lynchings in the US between Reconstruction and World War II.

### State-Sponsored Participatory Systems

State-sponsored participatory persecution represents the most systematic and institutionalized form, where governments create legal and social frameworks that require or encourage citizen participation in monitoring, reporting, and punishing targeted individuals.

**North Korea - Institutionalized Participatory Persecution:** The persecution of Christians and other dissenting groups in North Korea involves extensive citizen participation. The country's legal framework and social control systems create a pervasive informer network where citizens are compelled to report suspicious activities. According to Human Rights Watch's 2026 World Report, North Korea has strengthened laws to increase citizen reporting of criminal or suspicious activities, alongside mass surveillance measures. Border guards operate under a "shoot-on-sight" order for unauthorized border crossers, and the Ministry of State Security intensified crackdowns on cross-border communication in December 2024. The persecution extends to family members of Christians, with children as young as two years old being detained. Public executions serve as both punishment and deterrent, with Ri Hyon-ok allegedly publicly executed in 2009 for distributing Bibles, while her husband and children were deported to political prison camps. The system creates a climate where participation in persecution becomes a social obligation and survival mechanism.

**China - Social Credit and Mass Surveillance:** China's social credit system and mass surveillance infrastructure represent a sophisticated form of state-sponsored participatory persecution. The system, which consolidates municipal pilots into a national platform, enables cross-province enforcement and real-time monitoring of citizens' behavior. According to legal analysis from 2025, social credit blacklisting can convert apparent administrative harassment into evidence of systematic persecution when properly documented. The Upper Tribunal in QH (China) v SSHD (2024) accepted that systematic blacklisting amounts to persecution where the underlying conduct involves protected activities such as religious practice, political expression, or membership of an ethnic minority. The system relies on citizen reporting and neighbor monitoring, with approximately 200 million video surveillance cameras installed nationwide as part of the Skynet project, capable of real-time pedestrian tracking and recognition.

**Law Enforcement Integration:** In North Korea, the Ministry of Social Security and Ministry of State Security maintain pervasive systems of informers throughout society, with surveillance of citizens being routine. The State Security Department carries out counterintelligence and internal security functions, searching out "antistate criminals" including those accused of anti-government activities, economic crimes, and slander of political leadership. The integration of party apparatus with security structures ensures that citizen participation in persecution is both systematic and ideologically aligned with state objectives.

### Collective Punishment and Guilt by Association

Collective punishment mechanisms extend participatory persecution beyond direct participants to entire families, communities, or social groups, creating a multiplier effect that amplifies the impact of individual persecution.

**North Korea - Family Responsibility System:** North Korea's persecution apparatus explicitly targets family members of dissenters. When authorities discover that deported refugees have converted to Christianity, they subject them to harsher treatment, torture, and prolonged imprisonment. The Korea Future Initiative found that Christians are disproportionally imprisoned compared to North Koreans of other faiths, with estimates of 50,000-70,000 Christians held in political prison camps. The family responsibility system ensures that the children, spouses, and extended family of perceived dissidents face similar persecution, creating a powerful deterrent against individual dissent.

**Vigilante Collective Punishment in Nigeria:** The vigilante attacks on Fulani herders in Nigeria's Agwara district demonstrate collective punishment at the community level. Vigilantes from the Kambari and Bargawa ethnic groups accused all Fulani herders of complicity in jihadist violence, leading to indiscriminate killings, looting, and home destruction. This form of collective punishment, where entire ethnic groups are targeted based on the alleged actions of some members, represents a decentralized but systematic form of participatory persecution that operates outside state structures but with significant societal participation.

## Open Questions

1. **Conceptual Definition:** Does "participatory persecution" require a formal definition distinct from existing frameworks of collective violence, mob violence, or state-sponsored persecution?

2. **Digital-Physical Convergence:** How do digital participatory persecution campaigns transition to physical violence, and what are the specific mechanisms that facilitate this escalation?

3. **State Responsibility:** To what extent can states be held accountable for participatory persecution carried out by non-state actors when state rhetoric or policy creates enabling environments?

4. **Measurement Challenges:** How can the scale and impact of participatory persecution be systematically measured, particularly in digital contexts where participation may be diffuse and difficult to track?

5. **Prevention Strategies:** What are the most effective intervention points for preventing participatory persecution in its various forms, and how do these differ across digital, physical, and state-sponsored contexts?

6. **Legal Frameworks:** Do existing international human rights legal frameworks adequately address participatory persecution, or are new legal categories needed?

## Recommendations

**For Researchers:**
- Develop a comprehensive theoretical framework that explicitly defines participatory persecution and distinguishes it from related concepts
- Conduct comparative studies across different forms of participatory persecution to identify common mechanisms and risk factors
- Establish systematic monitoring and documentation methodologies for digital participatory persecution

**For Policymakers:**
- Strengthen legal frameworks to address the specific challenges of digital participatory persecution, including cross-border jurisdiction issues
- Develop early warning systems for detecting and preventing mob violence, particularly in contexts of social media amplification
- Create accountability mechanisms for state actors that enable or encourage participatory persecution

**For Technology Companies:**
- Implement more robust content moderation and account verification systems to prevent coordinated harassment campaigns
- Develop detection mechanisms for swatting, doxxing, and deepfake-based harassment
- Establish rapid response protocols for addressing emerging participatory persecution tactics

**For Civil Society:**
- Build resilience and digital literacy programs to help vulnerable groups protect against participatory persecution
- Establish support networks for victims of participatory persecution, including legal, psychological, and social support
- Develop counter-narrative strategies to combat disinformation and hate speech that fuel participatory persecution
