### Historical and Political Campaigns of Collective Persecution

#### **East Asian Contexts**
- **Cultural Revolution (文化大革命, Wénhuà Dà Gémìng)**
  The Chinese campaign (1966–1976) of mass denunciations, public humiliation, and violence against perceived "class enemies," often driven by ideological zeal and state manipulation.

- **Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries (镇压反革命运动, Zhèn yā Fǎn Gémìng Yùndòng)**
  Early PRC campaign (1950–1953) involving mass arrests and executions of alleged counterrevolutionaries, with widespread denunciations and forced confessions.

- **Denunciation Movement (控诉运动, Kòngsù Yùndòng)**
  Organized public accusation meetings in 1950s China, where individuals were pressured to publicly denounce others, often under coercion.

---

#### Soviet and Eastern European Contexts
- **Great Purge / Great Terror (Большая чистка, Bol'shaya chistka / Большой террор, Bol'shoy terror)**
  Stalin’s campaign (1936–1938) of political repression, featuring show trials, forced confessions, and mass executions of perceived enemies, including party members, military officers, and intellectuals.
  - **Yezhovshchina (Ежовщина)**
    Named after Nikolai Yezhov, head of the NKVD, this term refers specifically to the peak period of the purge under his leadership, characterized by extreme paranoia and arbitrary arrests.

- **Zersetzung (German: "decomposition")**
  A psychological warfare technique employed by the **Stasi** (East Germany’s secret police) in the 1970s–1980s. It involved covert psychological manipulation—such as smear campaigns, career sabotage, and social isolation—to destabilize dissidents and perceived enemies of the state, often using a vast network of **inoffizielle Mitarbeiter** (informal collaborators/informants).

---

#### **Western Historical Contexts**
- **Reign of Terror (la Terreur / la Grande Terreur)**
  The period during the French Revolution (1793–1794) when the **Jacobin** government, led by Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety, executed thousands of perceived enemies of the Revolution through **Revolutionary Tribunals** and the **Law of Suspects**. The term *Jacobinism* later became synonymous with radical, centralized revolutionary rule.

- **Witch Hunts / Witch Purges**
  Historical campaigns (15th–18th centuries in Europe and colonial America) targeting individuals accused of witchcraft, often driven by moral panic, mass hysteria, and religious zeal. The **Salem Witch Trials** (1692) in Massachusetts are a notable example.
  - **Modern Usage**
    The term is now metaphorically applied to any campaign of persecution driven by moral panic, false accusations, or ideological targeting (e.g., **McCarthyism** in 1950s America, **Stalinist witch-hunts**, **McCarthyite witch-hunts**).

- **Red Scare**
  Periods of intense anti-communist fear and persecution in the United States (1919–1920, 1947–1957), led by figures like Senator Joseph McCarthy, involving blacklisting, investigations, and the suppression of suspected communists.

- **Lynching**
  Extrajudicial killings by mobs, historically prevalent in the United States (particularly against African Americans) and in other societies during periods of instability. In Europe, lynching of political enemies occurred during revolutions (e.g., French Revolution, 1848 Revolutions). Contemporary **mob justice** and **lynch mobs** persist in some regions, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, where accusations of witchcraft often lead to violent collective action.

### Sociological and Psychological Frameworks

#### **Collective Behavior Terms**
- **Moral Panic**
  A widespread feeling of fear that a person, group, or behavior threatens societal values, often amplified by media and authorities. Examples include the **Satanic Panic** (1980s–1990s) and historical witch hunts. Moral panics frequently lead to scapegoating and collective persecution.

- **Mass Hysteria**
  A psychological phenomenon where fear or anxiety spreads rapidly through a group, leading to irrational or extreme behavior. Historical examples include the **Salem Witch Trials** and **Dancing Plague of 1518**.

- **Collective Violence**
  Violent behavior engaged in by large groups, ranging from spontaneous **riots** to organized forms like **coups**, **rebellions**, or **war**. Criminologists categorize it as situational, organized, or institutional.

- **Mob Mentality / Crowd Psychology**
  The tendency of individuals in a crowd to lose self-awareness and conform to group behavior, often leading to violence or irrational acts. Theorists like **Gustave Le Bon** (*The Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind*, 1895) described how crowds can become "unanimous, emotional, and intellectually weak," enabling primitive or hedonistic behavior.

- **Contagion Theory**
  An early sociological explanation for collective behavior, suggesting that individuals in a crowd become "infected" by a **mob mentality** and lose the ability to reason independently.

#### Psychological Mechanisms
- **Deindividuation**
  A state where individuals in a group lose their sense of personal identity and responsibility, making them more likely to engage in antisocial or violent behavior. This concept is often used to explain **lynch mobs**, **witch hunts**, and online harassment.

- **Groupthink**
  A psychological phenomenon where group cohesion and the desire for harmony override realistic appraisal of alternatives, leading to irrational or harmful decisions. Historically linked to failures like the **Bay of Pigs Invasion** or **Nazi Germany’s invasion of the Soviet Union**.

- **Scapegoating**
  The practice of blaming and punishing a person or group for problems they did not cause, often to deflect guilt or unify a community. Scapegoating has been used to explain events from the **execution of "witches"** in early modern Europe to **20th-century genocides** like the Holocaust.

- Diffusion of Responsibility
  A psychological effect where individuals in a group feel less personal accountability for their actions, enabling collective violence or harm.

### Mechanisms and Processes

- **Denunciation (控诉, Kòngsù / Денонсация, Denonsatsiya)**
  The act of publicly accusing or reporting individuals to authorities, often under coercion or ideological pressure. Central to the **Cultural Revolution**, **Great Purge**, and **Reign of Terror**.

- **Show Trials**
  Publicly staged judicial proceedings where the outcome is predetermined, often used to legitimize persecution. Examples include the **Moscow Trials** (1936–1938) and trials during the **Reign of Terror**.

- **Struggle Sessions (批斗会, Pīdòu Huì)**
  Public meetings in Maoist China where individuals were humiliated, criticized, and physically abused by crowds, often as part of ideological campaigns.

- **Accusation Meetings**
  Organized gatherings where individuals were pressured to confess or denounce others, as seen in the **Denunciation Movement** in 1950s China.

- **Law of Suspects (Loi des suspect, 1793)**
  A decree during the French Revolution that allowed the arrest of anyone suspected of opposing the Revolution, leading to mass denunciations and executions.

### Terms for Informants and Collaborators

- **Stool Pigeon**
  An informant who infiltrates a group to secretly supply authorities with information. The term originates from the practice of using a decoy pigeon tied to a stool to lure other birds.

- **Snitch / Rat / Canary / Grass (British slang) / Tout**
  Pejorative terms for informants, often used in criminal or political contexts.

- **Inoffizielle Mitarbeiter (IM)**
  The East German Stasi’s term for its network of **informal collaborators**, numbering between 170,000 and 500,000, who spied on fellow citizens as part of **Zersetzung** operations.

- **Collaborator**
  A neutral or pejorative term for someone who cooperates with occupying forces or oppressive regimes, often used in the context of **Nazi-occupied Europe** or **Stalinist purges**.

### **Contemporary and Cross-Cultural Manifestations**

- **Mob Justice**
  A form of extrajudicial punishment where crowds take the law into their own hands, often resulting in lynchings or public humiliation. Common in regions like **Sub-Saharan Africa**, where accusations of witchcraft frequently lead to violent collective action. Terms like **"witch camps"** (Ghana) describe sanctuaries for those fleeing persecution.

- **Cancel Culture**
  A modern digital-age phenomenon where individuals or groups are publicly shamed, boycotted, or ostracized for perceived wrongdoing, often through social media campaigns. Critics argue it shares mechanisms with historical witch hunts, including **moral panic** and **scapegoating**.

- **Political Witch Hunt**
  A metaphorical term for campaigns targeting political opponents with exaggerated or false accusations, often driven by ideological motives. Examples include **McCarthyism** in the U.S. and **Stalinist purges** in the USSR.

- **Vigilantism**
  Self-appointed enforcement of justice by citizens, often leading to mob violence or extrajudicial killings. Historical examples include **lynching** in the American South and contemporary **witch hunts** in Africa and Asia.
