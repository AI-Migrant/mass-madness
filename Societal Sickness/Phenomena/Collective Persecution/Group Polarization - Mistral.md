# Group Polarization in the UK: A Deep Dive with Case Studies

---

## Question
This report explores the phenomenon of group polarization in the United Kingdom, focusing on its causes, mechanisms, and real-world consequences. The primary research question is:

How has group polarization manifested in the UK in recent years, and what are the key drivers and case studies that illustrate this phenomenon?

---

## Executive Summary

1. Group polarization in the UK has been most prominently exemplified by the Brexit referendum and its aftermath, where affective polarization—defined as the emotional divide between in-groups and out-groups—intensified significantly.

2. The Brexit case study reveals that ideological polarization, particularly around cultural issues such as national identity and immigration, was the primary driver of affective polarization, rather than partisan identity.

3. Elite behavior, including emotionally charged rhetoric and negative media campaigning, deepened the divide between Leave and Remain supporters, leading to real-world consequences such as a surge in hate crimes.

4. Recent studies indicate that polarization in the UK is not limited to Brexit. Attitudes towards immigration have also become more polarized, with growing divides between political parties, age groups, and educational backgrounds.

5. Local-level studies highlight how economic and geographic polarization, driven by inequality, further exacerbate social and political divisions in communities across the UK.

6. Interventions such as befriending meditation have shown modest success in reducing affective polarization by increasing perceived commonality between opposing groups.

7. The enduring legacy of Brexit identities suggests that polarization in the UK is not merely a temporary phenomenon but a structural shift in how political and social identities are formed.

## Methodology

### Scope and Focus
This report focuses on recent phenomena (2016–2024) in the UK, with a deep dive into 2-3 case studies to provide a detailed understanding of group polarization. The analysis prioritizes Brexit as the primary case study, supplemented by insights from studies on immigration attitudes and local-level polarization.

### Search Strategy
The research was conducted using a combination of academic and reputable sources, including:
- Peer-reviewed journal articles (e.g., *Sigma: Journal of Political and International Studies*, *PLOS One*, *British Journal of Political Science*).
- Reports from think tanks and research organizations (e.g., MHP Group, National Centre for Social Research).
- News articles and policy briefs (e.g., UK Parliament POST notes).

### Source Selection
Sources were selected based on their relevance to the UK context, recency (2016–2024), and depth of analysis. Priority was given to studies that provided empirical evidence, case studies, or quantitative data on polarization.

### Analytical Framework
The report employs the following frameworks to analyze polarization:
- **Affective Polarization**: Measured as the emotional divide between in-groups and out-groups (e.g., Remainers vs. Leavers).
- **Ideological Polarization**: Driven by policy preferences on cultural or economic issues.
- **Elite Behavior**: The role of political leaders and media in amplifying polarization.
- **Socioeconomic Correlates**: The relationship between polarization and factors such as inequality, education, and age.

## Findings

### Case Study 1: Brexit and Affective Polarization

#### Overview
The 2016 Brexit referendum marked a turning point in UK politics, transforming a previously low-salience issue (EU membership) into a deeply polarizing one. The referendum resulted in a 52% vote to leave the EU, but its most enduring impact was the affective polarization it unleashed. Two years after the vote, 42% of British citizens reported that criticism of their side (Leave or Remain) felt like a personal insult (Evans and Schaffner, 2019).

#### Causes of Polarization
1. **Ideological Divide on Cultural Issues**:
   - The Brexit debate was not primarily driven by partisan identity but by ideological differences, particularly on cultural issues such as national identity and immigration.
   - A survey conducted on the day of the referendum found that 82% of Leave voters cited cultural concerns—such as the principle of UK sovereignty or regaining control over immigration—as their primary motivators (Ashcroft, 2016).
   - Cultural issues are more likely to generate intense polarization because they intertwine with deeply held identities (Gidron, Adams, and Horne, 2022).

2. **Elite Behavior and Negative Campaigning**:
   - Elite politicians and media played a significant role in amplifying polarization. Negative rhetoric from both Remain and Leave campaigns, including misinformation and emotionally charged messaging, deepened the divide.
   - For example, the Leave campaign's slogan "Take Back Control" resonated with voters concerned about immigration, while controversial posters (e.g., depicting Syrian refugees with the caption "Breaking point") stoked fears about migration (Stewart and Mason, 2016).
   - The murder of Labour MP Jo Cox, a Remain supporter, by a far-right extremist a week before the referendum highlighted the extreme consequences of affective polarization (Cobain, Parveen, and Taylor, 2016).

3. **Lack of Partisan Unity**:
   - Unlike in the US, where polarization is often driven by partisan identity, the UK's major political parties (Conservative and Labour) were internally divided over Brexit. This lack of party unity meant that partisan identity was not the primary driver of polarization.
   - Instead, Brexit-based identities (Leave vs. Remain) became the dominant polarizing force, with people more likely to identify with these labels than with traditional party affiliations (Hobolt, Leeper, and Tilley, 2021).

#### Consequences
- **Hate Crimes**: Hate crimes against minorities and Eastern Europeans increased by 42% during the two weeks surrounding the Brexit vote (Dodd, 2016).
- **Identity Entrenchment**: Five years after the referendum, Brexit remained the most polarizing issue in the UK, with 40% of Britons identifying as either "very strong Remainers" or "very strong Leavers" (Curtice et al., 2019).
- **Media Distrust**: The referendum led to a situation where both sides accused the other of spreading misinformation, eroding trust in institutions and experts (Easton, 2016).

#### Post-Brexit Trends
- The Conservative Party's consolidation around a pro-Brexit stance after the referendum helped reduce some of the conflict between Brexit and partisan identities. However, the enduring legacy of Brexit identities suggests that affective polarization is likely to persist.
- Polarization has also shifted from Leave vs. Remain identities to Conservative vs. Labour divisions, with Brexit becoming a symbolic watershed moment in UK politics (MHP Group, 2021).

### Case Study 2: Polarization in Attitudes Towards Immigration

#### Overview
Attitudes towards immigration in the UK have become increasingly polarized, despite an overall trend towards more liberal views between 2014 and 2021. The National Centre for Social Research (NatCen) reports that while positive attitudes towards immigration increased during this period, the divide between different demographic and political groups also widened.

#### Key Trends
1. **Liberalization and Polarization**:
   - Between 2014 and 2021, the proportion of people who believed migrants were good for the economy increased from 30% to 59%. Similarly, the percentage who thought immigration made Britain a better place to live rose from 29% to 55%.
   - However, this liberalization was accompanied by growing polarization. For example, the gap between younger (15–24) and older (75+) age groups in their support for allowing more people from the same racial and ethnic group to come to Britain widened from 16 points in 2010 to 42 points in 2021.

2. **Political Divides**:
   - The gap between Conservative and Labour supporters in their attitudes towards immigration has also widened. In 2011, 15% of Conservative supporters and 29% of Labour supporters believed migrants were good for the economy (a 14-point gap). By 2021, this gap had increased to 30 points (35% of Conservatives vs. 65% of Labour supporters).
   - Recent data (2023) shows that this gap remains at 29 points, with positive views of immigration decreasing slightly among both parties.

3. **Policy Disagreements**:
   - Conservative and Labour supporters disagree on key immigration policies. For example:
     - 74% of Labour supporters believe the NHS should be free to recruit as many doctors and nurses from overseas as it wants, while 54% of Conservatives believe the government should limit such recruitment.
     - 70% of Conservative supporters believe there should be an income requirement of over £28,000 to bring a spouse or family member into the UK, compared to 71% of Labour supporters who believe the requirement should be lower.

#### Drivers of Polarization
- **Demographic Factors**: Younger people and those with higher educational qualifications are more likely to hold liberal views on immigration, while older people and those with fewer qualifications are more likely to hold restrictive views.
- **Political Identity**: The alignment of immigration attitudes with political party affiliation has strengthened, contributing to the polarization of views.
- **External Events**: The COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent economic challenges may have contributed to the recent reversal of the liberalization trend, with positive attitudes towards immigration declining slightly since 2021.

### Case Study 3: Local-Level Polarization

#### Overview
Polarization in the UK is not only a national phenomenon but also manifests at the local level, where economic and geographic inequalities drive social and political divisions. A comparative study of four UK towns (Oxford, Tunbridge Wells, Margate, and Oldham) highlights how polarization takes different forms depending on local contexts (Koch et al., 2021).

#### Key Findings
1. **Economic and Geographic Polarization**:
   - In Oxford and Tunbridge Wells, polarization is driven by the presence of a large, wealthy elite population, with socially deprived communities pushed to the periphery. This is described as "elite-driven" polarization.
   - In Margate and Oldham, polarization is driven by high levels of poverty and a large precariat (a class of people in precarious employment). This is described as "poverty-driven" polarization.

2. **Relational Polarization**:
   - Across all four towns, marginalized communities express a sense of local belonging, but this is often overshadowed by the dominance of elite or precariat groups.
   - The study highlights how economic inequality can lead to the peripheralization of marginalized groups, further exacerbating social divisions.

3. **Political Consequences**:
   - The differing dynamics of polarization in these towns make it difficult to build broad-based political coalitions, as the "middle" (moderate voices) is often small compared to the extremes.
   - This polarization can lead to the entrenchment of political and social attitudes, making it harder to address local inequalities.

#### Implications
- Local-level polarization underscores the importance of addressing economic inequality as a means of reducing social and political divisions.
- The study suggests that polarization is not solely a top-down phenomenon driven by national politics but is also shaped by local economic and social conditions.

## Mechanisms of Polarization

### Affective Polarization
Affective polarization occurs when individuals develop increasingly positive feelings toward their own group (in-group) and increasingly negative feelings toward opposing groups (out-group). This phenomenon is measured as the difference between how much an individual prefers their own group and how much they dislike the other group (Gidron, Adams, and Horne, 2020).

In the UK, affective polarization has been particularly evident in the Brexit debate, where Remainers and Leavers have come to see each other as fundamentally different and even hostile. This emotional divide is often more intense than disagreements over policy, as it taps into deeply held identities and values.

### Ideological Polarization
Ideological polarization refers to the process whereby groups' policy preferences move consistently farther apart, often leading to more extreme positions. In the UK, ideological polarization has been most evident in debates over cultural issues such as national identity and immigration, which are more likely to evoke strong emotions than economic issues (Gidron, Adams, and Horne, 2022).

### Elite Behavior and Media
Elite politicians and media outlets play a critical role in amplifying polarization. Negative campaigning, emotionally charged rhetoric, and the spread of misinformation can deepen divisions by reinforcing in-group/out-group dynamics. For example, during the Brexit referendum, both Remain and Leave campaigns engaged in negative messaging that portrayed the opposing side as dishonest or even dangerous.

### Social and Economic Inequality
Polarization is often linked to broader social and economic inequalities. In the UK, rising income inequality since the 1980s has contributed to geographic and relational polarization, with marginalized communities feeling increasingly disconnected from more prosperous areas. This economic polarization can reinforce political and social divisions, creating a feedback loop that is difficult to break.

## Interventions and Mitigation

### Befriending Meditation
A study published in *PLOS One* (Simonsson et al., 2022) investigated the effects of a brief befriending meditation on affective polarization among Remainers and Leavers. The study found that participants who listened to a 10-minute guided befriending meditation scored modestly lower on affective polarization than those in a control condition. The effect was mediated by increased perceived commonality with the political outgroup, suggesting that interventions targeting shared humanity can reduce polarization.

While the effect sizes were small, the study highlights the potential of scalable, low-cost interventions to address affective polarization. The authors suggest that meditation-based apps, which are widely popular, could be a viable means of delivering such interventions at scale.

### Policy Recommendations
1. **Addressing Economic Inequality**: Policies aimed at reducing income inequality and improving economic opportunities for marginalized communities could help mitigate the socioeconomic drivers of polarization.

2. **Promoting Cross-Group Dialogue**: Initiatives that encourage interaction and dialogue between polarized groups, such as deliberative polling or community engagement programs, may help reduce affective polarization by fostering mutual understanding.

3. **Media Literacy and Regulation**: Efforts to combat misinformation and promote media literacy could help reduce the role of elite rhetoric and negative campaigning in amplifying polarization. Additionally, regulations that limit the spread of harmful content on social media platforms may be beneficial.

4. **Political Reform**: Reforms that reduce the incentives for political parties to engage in polarizing rhetoric, such as changes to electoral systems or campaign finance laws, could help depolarize the political environment.

5. **Education and Awareness**: Educational programs that highlight the shared values and common humanity between opposing groups could help reduce affective polarization, particularly among younger generations.

## Open Questions

1. **Long-Term Effects of Brexit Polarization**: While Brexit identities remain strong, it is unclear how long affective polarization will persist and whether it will continue to shape UK politics in the long term.

2. **Effectiveness of Interventions**: The study on befriending meditation showed modest effects. Further research is needed to determine whether such interventions can have a lasting impact on reducing polarization.

3. **Role of Social Media**: The role of social media in amplifying polarization in the UK is not yet fully understood. Future research could explore how algorithms and online echo chambers contribute to affective polarization.

4. **Cross-National Comparisons**: How does polarization in the UK compare to other countries, particularly those with similar political and social contexts? Are there lessons to be learned from countries that have successfully reduced polarization?

5. **Economic vs. Cultural Drivers**: While cultural issues such as immigration and national identity have been key drivers of polarization, the extent to which economic factors (e.g., inequality, job insecurity) contribute to polarization remains an open question.

## Recommendations

### For Policymakers
- Implement policies that address economic inequality and marginalization, as these are key drivers of polarization at both the national and local levels.
- Support initiatives that promote cross-group dialogue and understanding, such as deliberative polling or community engagement programs.
- Regulate media and social media platforms to limit the spread of misinformation and harmful rhetoric that amplifies polarization.

### For Researchers
- Conduct further studies on the long-term effects of Brexit polarization and the effectiveness of interventions such as befriending meditation.
- Explore the role of social media in amplifying polarization and develop strategies to mitigate its impact.
- Investigate cross-national comparisons to identify best practices for reducing polarization in other countries.

### For the Public
- Engage in open and respectful dialogue with individuals from opposing political or social groups to foster mutual understanding.
- Seek out diverse sources of information to avoid echo chambers and reduce the likelihood of affective polarization.
- Support organizations and initiatives that work to bridge divides and promote social cohesion.

---

## Conclusion
Group polarization in the UK is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon, driven by a combination of ideological, affective, and socioeconomic factors. The Brexit referendum serves as a clear illustration of how cultural issues, elite behavior, and media rhetoric can deepen divisions within society. While recent studies on immigration attitudes and local-level polarization highlight the enduring nature of these divides, interventions such as befriending meditation offer hope for reducing affective polarization.

Addressing polarization in the UK will require a multi-pronged approach that includes policy reforms, media regulation, and initiatives to promote cross-group dialogue and understanding. By tackling the root causes of polarization—such as economic inequality and cultural divides—policymakers and civil society can work towards a more cohesive and inclusive society.
