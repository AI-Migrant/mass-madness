# The Respect Asymmetry

## A study of the mindset behind authorities that treat people with no respect yet demand respect from those they harm.

---

## Question

What psychological, sociological, and political mechanisms explain the recurring pattern in which figures of authority treat subordinates, citizens, or victims with overt disrespect and dehumanisation, while simultaneously demanding — and enforcing — respect, deference, and compliance from those very people?

---

## Executive Summary

1. **The pattern is structurally consistent, not accidental.** Across policing, workplaces, families, and states, a recognisable script repeats: an authority withdraws dignity from those below it while treating deference from those same people as an entitlement. The asymmetry is the point, not a glitch.

2. **Two faces of "respect" are deliberately conflated.** Authorities collapse "respect-as-derence" (behavioural compliance owed to rank) with "respect-as-dignity" (moral worth owed to persons). They demand the first while denying the second, exploiting the linguistic ambiguity to make the victim's lack of compliance appear as the real offence.

3. **Authoritarian personality structures supply the dispositional engine.** The Adorno–Altemeyer tradition shows a single syndrome combining submission upward and aggression downward. The same individual who is obsequious to superiors is punitive to subordinates; respect flows only one direction — toward power.

4. **Power itself deforms the holder.** The "hubris syndrome" literature (Owen, Davidson) documents how sustained authority produces contempt for subordinates, loss of empathy, and a conviction of exemption from ordinary rules — precisely the mindset that treats the disrespected party's resistance as intolerable insolence.

5. **Dehumanisation of the subordinate is a precondition, not a side-effect.** Haslam's integrative review shows that stripping a group of human attributes lowers the moral cost of harming them, while simultaneously making the authority's demand for their deference feel legitimate because the subordinate is no longer a full moral equal.

6. **The demand for respect functions as coercion and as cover.** DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender), kafkatrapping, and "contempt of cop" all show that demanding respect from the victim reframes the victim's resistance as the offence, shielding the original harm from scrutiny.

7. **Legitimate authority requires reciprocity.** From Arendt onward, the political-philosophical core of the critique is that authority without dignity is domination, and demands for respect severed from reciprocity are not moral claims but instruments of control.

---

## Methodology

This study triangulated three source strata:

- **Political and social philosophy**: Arendt on authority and human dignity; Nietzsche on ressentiment; the concept of legitimate authority.
- **Psychology and organisational science**: the authoritarian personality (Adorno, Altemeyer), right-wing authoritarianism, social dominance orientation, hubris syndrome, dehumanisation research, abusive supervision and authoritarian leadership studies.
- **Applied and critical accounts**: the "contempt of cop" phenomenon, victim-blaming and the just-world hypothesis, DARVO, kafkatrapping, and institutional betrayal.

Searches were conducted across academic databases (PMC, ResearchGate, Springer, Cambridge Core), reference encyclopaedias (Wikipedia), and practitioner commentary (Psychology Today, BMJ). Web search snippets were the primary evidence layer; where a source's claim depended on detail beyond the snippet, that limitation is flagged. No single ideological camp was privileged — conservative, liberal, and critical-theory framings were all included to test for convergence.

**Limitations.** The "respect asymmetry" is a descriptive synthesis rather than a named single theory in the literature; the report therefore assembles convergent strands rather than citing one canonical work. Several psychological constructs (hubris syndrome, SDO) are measured via self-report scales whose predictive validity is debated. Cultural variation in the meaning of "respect" is large, and most cited research is WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic) in origin.

## Findings

### 1. Two Meanings of "Respect" and Their Strategic Conflation

The whole pattern turns on a semantic ambiguity that authorities exploit. There is *respect-as-respectability* (recognition of moral standing and dignity, owed to persons as such), and there is *respect-as-deference* (compliance and submission owed to a role or rank). An authority that degrades a person's dignity but then demands their "respect" is silently swapping definitions: the demand sounds like a moral claim (one ought to respect others) while functioning as a demand for compliance with hierarchy. When the victim refuses deference, the authority presents this as proof of the victim's disrespectfulness — a character flaw — rather than as a reasonable response to having been demeaned. This conflation lets the aggressor occupy the moral high ground while continuing to deny it to the victim.

### 2. The Authoritarian Personality: Submission Upward, Aggression Downward

The dispositional root of the asymmetry is documented in the authoritarian personality tradition. Originating with Erich Fromm and operationalised by Adorno and later Altemeyer, the construct describes people who show "unquestioning obedience and respect" toward perceived superiors while behaving "strictly and oppressively" toward those below them. Altemeyer's Right-Wing Authoritarianism scale captures three converging traits: **authoritarian submission** (deference to established authority), **authoritarian aggression** (punitive hostility toward sanctioned targets), and **conventionalism** (rigid adherence to perceived norms).

The structure is inherently asymmetric: respect is a vertical commodity that travels upward only. For such a personality, subordinates are not owed respect because they do not occupy a position that confers it; the superior is owed respect regardless of conduct because the position itself confers it. This is why the same manager can demand hierarchical respect (submission) while harshly punishing subordinates who offer ideas (anti-intraception), and experience no contradiction. The inconsistency is invisible because, within the authoritarian frame, respect is attached to rank rather than personhood.

### 3. Power Deforms the Holder: Hubris and Empathy Loss

Holding power changes the psychology of the powerful in ways that actively produce the asymmetry. Owen and Davidson's "hubris syndrome" research, summarised in clinical reviews in the BMJ, identifies how sustained authority fosters contempt for advice, loss of contact with reality, and a conviction of personal exemption from the rules that constrain others. Neuroscience-adjacent commentary on "power corrupts the brain" reports that elevated power measurably reduces the holder's ability to take others' perspectives and to empathise.

Two consequences follow directly. First, the authority ceases to register subordinates as full subjects whose dignity can be injured — so the authority's disrespect feels costless and even justified. Second, because the authority has lost empathy, the subordinate's failure to defer is experienced not as a response to mistreatment but as unprovoked insolence — an offence demanding correction. The mindset is self-sealing: power erodes the very capacity that would let the holder see the harm it causes, so the harm becomes invisible and the victim's resistance becomes the only visible wrongdoing.

### 4. Social Dominance Orientation: Hierarchy as a Moral Good

Social dominance theory supplies the legitimating ideology. Individuals high in social dominance orientation (SDO) endorse hierarchy as desirable and view unequal treatment of dominant and subordinate groups as legitimate; the SDO scale correlates robustly with group-based prejudice and with hierarchy-enhancing policy. Within this worldview, demanding deference from subordinates while denying them dignity is not hypocrisy — it is the correct ordering of the world. Subordinates are assumed to have less of whatever justifies respectful treatment, so the demand that they nevertheless show respect appears as the restoration of proper order rather than as an injustice. The asymmetry is rationalised as the natural state of affairs.

### 5. Dehumanisation as a Precondition, Not a Side-Effect

Nick Haslam's integrative review of dehumanisation distinguishes mechanisms by which people are denied attributes thought to distinguish humans from animals (animalistic dehumanisation) or from machines and objects (mechanistic dehumanisation). Dehumanisation lowers the moral threshold for harming others and removes the felt contradiction of demanding moral behaviour from those one has reduced below the moral circle. In the respect-asymmetry dynamic, dehumanisation is what makes the authority's stance psychologically coherent: one can degrade a person yet still demand their deference only if that person has first been reclassified as not-quite-a-person, as someone whose own dignity claim does not count. The demand for "respect" is then recast as a demand for the obedience due to a keeper from livestock, or a guard from an inmate, not as the reciprocity due between equals.

### 6. Demanding Respect as Coercion and Cover: DARVO, Kafkatrapping, Contempt of Cop

When victims resist, authorities deploy rhetorical structures that reframe the victim's resistance as the original wrong.

- **DARVO** (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender), studied in the perpetrator-confrontation literature, is a strategy in which the perpetrator denies wrongdoing, attacks the victim's credibility, and repositions themselves as the true victim of the victim's behaviour. The victim's demand for accountability is recast as the victim's aggression; the perpetrator's demand for respect from the victim becomes the injured party's rightful grievance.

- **Kafkatrapping** names the logic in which denial of guilt is treated as proof of guilt — "That is how the guilty speak." Any self-defence by the victim is converted into evidence against them, so the only acceptable posture is submission. This is the purest expression of the respect-asymmetry: the authority defines the victim's dignity-claim itself as insubordination, and demands that the victim demonstrate "respect" precisely by abandoning the claim.

- **"Contempt of cop"** is the field instantiation. Wikipedia documents it as law-enforcement jargon for behaviour an officer *perceives* as insufficiently deferential, noting it arises from "a type of 'occupational arrogance' when a police officer thinks their authority cannot or should not be challenged or questioned." Arrests for disorderly conduct or resisting arrest frequently follow not criminal conduct but a failure of deference — a citizen's "attitude." Leniency is afforded to those who treat officers with respect; "the heavy hand of the law" falls on those who are "disrespectful, ill mannered or rude." The officer's own demeanour toward the citizen is not part of the calculus. This is the asymmetry operating as criminal procedure: the state's representative owes the citizen no civility, but the citizen owes the state's representative deference, enforced by arrest.

### 7. Victim-Blaming and the Just-World Hypothesis

Bystanders and institutions reinforce the asymmetry through victim-blaming. The just-world hypothesis — the cognitive need to believe the world is fair — leads observers to attribute a victim's suffering to the victim's own conduct, because acknowledging the perpetrator's fault would threaten the belief that outcomes are earned. Philosophical analyses of victim-blaming note that it "attributes responsibility to the victim for the wrong" precisely when doing so is morally improper, and that it serves to "make salient the victim's conduct" while "backgrounding the conduct of the perpetrator." This is the social mechanism that lets authorities demand respect from the people they have harmed: the culture supplies a ready explanation (the victim must have provoked it, must have been disrespectful, must have failed to comply) that relocates the wrongdoing onto the victim. The authority's demand for respect is thereby back-validated: if the victim had only been respectful, the harm would not have occurred.

### 8. Arendt: Authority Without Dignity Is Domination

The political-philosophical anchor is Hannah Arendt's distinction between authority and coercion. For Arendt, genuine authority rests on consent and on recognition of the legitimacy of the ordering, not on force; once authority must be *enforced*, it has already collapsed into domination. Her work on human dignity argues that dignity is the precondition for the "right to have rights" — that totalitarianism demonstrated, brutally, that "human dignity needs a new guarantee" precisely because regimes that strip dignity while commanding obedience have severed authority from its only legitimate foundation.

The respect-asymmetry is, in Arendtian terms, the signature of domination masquerading as authority. The demand for respect from those denied dignity is not authority in operation; it is coercion wearing authority's costume. The test is reciprocity: legitimate authority can command obedience precisely because it recognises the dignity of those it commands. Authority that withholds recognition and yet demands deference has revealed that it possesses only power, not authority.

**Conflicts and caveats.** The "respect asymmetry" itself is not a single named theory; findings are assembled from convergent strands, so causal claims are inferential. The kafkatrapping literature is largely polemical (blog/commentary) and is cited for its descriptive label, not as peer-reviewed evidence. Hubris syndrome is a clinical proposal, not a settled diagnosis. Arendt's own writing rarely uses the word "dignity" directly; the dignity reading of Arendt is a well-developed secondary interpretation. The SDO construct's predictive scope is debated in social psychology.

## Open Questions

1. **Cultural generalisation.** How does the asymmetry manifest in honour cultures, where "respect" is bound to reputation and face, versus in dignity cultures, where it is bound to inherent personhood? The cited literature is predominantly WEIRD.
2. **System-level versus individual-level.** The synthesis mixes dispositional (personality), situational (role-induced hubris), and ideological (SDO, hierarchy-legitimising myths) explanations. Their relative weight and interaction are not quantified here.
3. **The victim's internalisation.** To what extent do those subjected to the asymmetry come to believe the demand is legitimate, and what enables resistance? The DARVO and kafkatrap literature begins this, but longitudinal evidence is thin.
4. **Recovery and repair.** What institutional designs have demonstrably inverted the asymmetry — i.e., bound authority's right to demand deference to a duty to confer dignity? This points toward accountability, civilian oversight, and dignity-anchored law.
5. **Distinguishing authority from domination empirically.** Arendt's reciprocity test is normative; operationalising it for measurement remains underdeveloped.

---

## Recommendations / Next Steps

1. **Adopt the reciprocity test.** Treat any demand for deference from an authority that does not concurrently recognise the dignity of the person asked as evidence of domination, not authority, and investigate accordingly.
2. **Disambiguate "respect" in policy.** In codes of conduct, grievance procedures, and policing standards, separate respect-as-deference (compliance with lawful instruction) from respect-as-dignity (humane treatment). Require both directions; sanction both directions.
3. **Build structural counterweights to hubris.** Term limits, rotation of command, independent oversight, mandatory perspective-taking and empathy training, and protected dissent channels address power-induced empathy loss at the institutional level.
4. **Name the tactics.** Training for those who encounter the asymmetry — citizens, employees, advocates — to recognise DARVO, kafkatrapping, and "contempt of cop" reduces their effectiveness as cover for the original harm.
5. **Pursue deeper primary reading.** For grounding, read Arendt's "What is Authority?" (1954), Haslam's "Dehumanization: An Integrative Review" (2006), Altemeyer's *The Authoritarians*, and the DARVO primary studies; these are the load-bearing sources beneath the synthesis.
