# UK Authorities and the Asymmetry of Respect

## Question

What documented cases show UK public authorities treating individuals with no respect, while simultaneously demanding respect, compliance, or deference from the very people they have mistreated? The scope covers all UK public authorities — policing, the Home Office and immigration enforcement, and the courts and criminal-justice system.

## Executive Summary

1. A recurring, evidence-backed pattern exists across UK public institutions: the state subjects people — often the most vulnerable — to degrading, contemptuous or abusive treatment, yet relies on laws and powers that criminalise "disrespect" directed back at the state or its officers.
2. The clearest legal embodiment is Section 5 of the Public Order Act 1986. Until 2014 it criminalised "insulting" words, and case law (notably *DPP v Orum*) confirmed that a police officer can be the "victim" of an insult. Officers could therefore arrest citizens for swearing at them, even as the same forces were committing serious misconduct against the public.
3. Policing provides the sharpest cases of the asymmetry: the Metropolitan Police strip-searched a 15-year-old schoolgirl ("Child Q") in her school and told her she "might be arrested" if she did not comply; officers later faced gross-misconduct findings. Days after one of their own (Wayne Couzens) murdered Sarah Everard, the same force arrested mourning women at the Clapham Common vigil and later paid damages and apologised.
4. The Home Office's "hostile environment" policy treated Windrush-generation British subjects as "guilty until proven innocent," detained and deported them, and made "irrational" documentary demands — while still requiring them to produce papers, attend interviews, and submit to detention. The Windrush Lessons Learned Review found "ignorance and thoughtlessness" and "complete disregard" for their rights.
5. Immigration detention (e.g. Brook House) saw officers using excessive force, racist and verbal abuse and humiliation of detainees — conduct an inquiry linked to Article 3 (inhuman/degrading treatment) concerns — while detainees remained legally required to comply with the regime abusing them.
6. The courts operate their own respect regime through contempt-in-the-face-of-the-court powers, which can imprison a person for disrespectful conduct toward a bench, even as miscarriages of justice and victim-blaming undermine the institution's own claim to respect.
7. The recurring structural feature is the power imbalance: authorities can compel deference through criminal law, while the individuals they harm have only reactive, after-the-fact complaints machinery (the IOPC, compensation schemes, judicial review) that is slow and frequently inadequate.

## Methodology

The investigation used iterative web search across six angles: (a) police misconduct and IOPC findings; (b) the Home Office "hostile environment" and Windrush; (c) European Court of Human Rights Article 3 jurisprudence; (d) public-order law and the criminalisation of "insulting" the police; (e) immigration-detention abuse; and (f) cases where victims were treated as suspects. Source types include statutory guidance and government statistics, inspectorate and inquiry reports, parliamentary material, court reporting, reputable news organisations, and human-rights NGOs. Limitations: the analysis relies on publicly available English-language reporting; internal Home Office and police records were not accessible; some source dates were unavailable; and individual case facts are summarised from secondary reporting rather than primary case files. The pattern described is a thematic synthesis of documented incidents, not a statistical claim that every authority interaction follows this pattern.

## Findings

### 1. The legal architecture: criminalising "insulting" the police

The most direct instrument is Section 5 of the Public Order Act 1986, which made it an offence to use "threatening, abusive or insulting words or behaviour" likely to cause "harassment, alarm or distress." The House of Commons Library notes Section 5 "has been used to arrest and/or prosecute … people who have sworn at the police." In *DPP v Orum* (1989) the Divisional Court confirmed that a police officer can be a person "caused harassment, alarm or distress" by swearing, provided the conduct is "in excess of what the officer is or should be used to." This created an explicit legal entitlement for officers to demand respectful language from the public.

The "Reform Section 5" campaign documented the chilling effect: a 16-year-old was summonsed for a placard reading "Scientology is not a religion it is a dangerous cult." Following sustained campaigning, the word "insulting" was removed from Section 5 by amendment (in force February 2014), though it remains in Section 4A. The College of Policing guidance on the 2013 amendment shows authorities resisted reform partly to retain powers over "swearing at police officers."

This is the structural core of the asymmetry: the state retains a criminal-law lever to compel respectful conduct toward its officers, even where those officers' own conduct toward the public is the subject of repeated misconduct findings.

### 2. Policing: abusing victims and demanding their compliance

**Child Q.** A 15-year-old Black schoolgirl was strip-searched by Metropolitan Police officers at her Hackney school without an appropriate adult present, while menstruating, after being suspected of cannabis possession (none was found). The IOPC found gross misconduct proven against two officers. Reporting records that she was told she "might be arrested" during the search — i.e. compliance was extracted under the threat of arrest — even as the search itself was found to be a serious wrong. The case is a textbook instance of humiliating treatment combined with a demand for submission.

**The Sarah Everard vigil (Clapham Common, 2021).** Women gathered to mourn Sarah Everard, murdered by serving Met officer Wayne Couzens. Police dispersed the vigil and arrested participants. HMICFRS inspected the policing; organisers won a legal challenge, and arrested women subsequently received payouts and an apology from the Met. The force demanded compliance with public-order restrictions from women mourning a woman killed by one of its own officers, while failing to prevent that killing.

**David Carrick and Wayne Couzens.** Carrick, a Met officer, was revealed as a serial rapist; an entire vetting system missed him. Victims of such officers were repeatedly disbelieved or poorly treated by the same institution that demands public deference. The Wikipedia article on "Institutional sexism in the Metropolitan Police" and the IOPC's findings on police abuse of power for sexual purpose document that abuse of power for sexual purpose is now the single largest category of police corruption referrals — officers exploiting the authority with which they are entrusted.

**Police abuse of power for sexual purpose.** The IOPC reported a "sharp rise" in cases; Sky News found officers in half of UK forces under investigation for abusing powers for sexual gain. The End Violence Against Women coalition noted the IOPC nonetheless placed responsibility on women to "speak up" — demanding action from victims of the very abuse.

**"Treated like a suspect."** Multiple rape complainants report being treated as suspects themselves. The Independent documented a rape victim who, after a three-year battle, stated "I was treated like a suspect." BBC reported a person who "had to investigate my own abuse case because the police failed me." The pattern: institutions demand cooperation and patience from victims while subjecting them to suspicion and delay.

### 3. The Home Office and immigration enforcement: "hostile environment"

**Windrush scandal.** The Home Office wrongly detained, denied rights, threatened with deportation, and in at least 83 cases wrongly deported British subjects of the Windrush generation. The Wikipedia summary and the Windrush Lessons Learned Review (Wendy Williams, March 2020) found the Home Office showed "ignorance and thoughtlessness," that the harm was "foreseeable and avoidable," and that regulations were tightened "with complete disregard for the Windrush generation," with officials making "irrational" demands for multiple documents per year of residence. Press reporting characterised the regime as "guilty until proven innocent" and "deport first, appeal later," cutting off access to jobs, healthcare and bank accounts while cases were still being investigated.

Throughout, the affected individuals were required to produce documentation, attend Home Office interviews, submit to detention, and prove entitlement to rights they already held — i.e. to perform deference and compliance to an authority that was acting unlawfully and "with complete disregard" toward them. By November 2021, only an estimated 5% of victims had received any compensation; 23 eligible individuals had died before payment. The Equality and Human Rights Commission found the hostile environment policy broke equalities law; the UN Human Rights Council said it fostered xenophobia.

**Immigration detention — Brook House.** A public inquiry (report published 2023/2024) found "pain and humiliation" at a "toxic" Brook House Immigration Removal Centre. Covert footage showed harsh treatment, bullying, racism and verbal abuse of detainees; Amnesty UK described people "routinely dehumanised and stripped of their rights." The EHRC had earlier warned the alleged abuse potentially breached Article 3 of the ECHR (prohibition of inhuman and degrading treatment). JRS UK reports that abuse "continue[s] today" and is "endemic" across the detention estate. Throughout, detainees remained legally subject to the authority of the very staff mistreating them — compliance was compulsory, respect was demanded, dignity was withheld.

### 4. The courts: contempt as a respect-enforcement power

Courts wield contempt-in-the-face-of-the-court jurisdiction, which can punish (including by imprisonment) disrespectful conduct toward a judge or court. This is the judicial analogue of Section 5: an institutional power to compel respect. The Law Commission and CPS guidance set out the framework. While this power is legitimately tied to maintaining order in proceedings, the asymmetry arises when the justice system itself fails victims or produces miscarriages — the institution demands respect it has not earned in individual cases. Reports of rape victims "treated like suspects," honour-based-abuse safeguarding failures flagged by HMICFRS, and documented police investigation failures show an institution that frequently withholds respectful, competent treatment while retaining coercive powers over those who appear before it.

### 5. The human-rights frame: Article 3 ECHR

Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights (incorporated via the Human Rights Act 1998) prohibits torture and inhuman or degrading treatment. It is an absolute right that places duties on UK authorities. The EHRC's description of Article 3 and the ECtHR's detention-case jurisprudence (summarised by Landmark Chambers) provide the benchmark against which Brook House-type conduct and detention conditions are measured. The recurring issue is not the absence of a legal standard requiring the state to treat people with dignity, but weak enforcement against authorities that nonetheless retain and exercise powers demanding respect from those same people.

## Open Questions

- How frequently is Section 5 (and Section 4A) used against individuals who were themselves victims of, or witnesses to, prior police misconduct in the same encounter? No dataset was found that joins these.
- What proportion of IOPC-substantiated misconduct cases result in meaningful sanction versus resignation/retirement before outcome? The IOPC outcomes report indicates four in five investigated cases are proven, but post-proven outcomes vary.
- Whether the Home Office's internal decision records show conscious trade-offs between enforcement targets and dignity obligations; these are not publicly available.
- The extent to which contempt powers are applied to litigants-in-person and vulnerable defendants disproportionately — limited public data.

2. Request IOPC and Home Office data on outcomes (sanction rates, compensation, time-to-resolution) to quantify the enforcement gap.
3. Consider a focused follow-up on a single authority type (e.g. immigration enforcement) if a sharper, advocacy-oriented brief is required.
4. Monitor the Crime and Policing Bill debates (2026) for any expansion or reform of public-order "respect" offences.
5. For litigation or advocacy, the Article 3 ECHR framework and the Human Rights Act remain the strongest levers to challenge degrading treatment while exposing the asymmetry documented above.
