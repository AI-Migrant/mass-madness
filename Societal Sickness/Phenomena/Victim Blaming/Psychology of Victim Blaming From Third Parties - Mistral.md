# Psychological Study of Victim Blaming from Third Parties

## Question

This research report investigates the psychological mechanisms, theories, and empirical evidence underlying victim blaming by third-party observers. Specifically, it examines why bystanders, witnesses, and other third parties attribute responsibility to victims for their misfortune, the cognitive and motivational processes involved, and the social and contextual factors that influence this phenomenon across different scenarios.

## Executive Summary

Victim blaming by third parties represents a pervasive social psychological phenomenon with deep roots in human cognition and motivation. The research reveals several key findings:

1. **Just World Hypothesis** stands as the most prominent explanatory framework, positing that individuals blame victims to preserve their belief in a just and orderly world where people receive what they deserve. This belief serves as a psychological coping mechanism that alleviates anxiety and maintains a sense of control and predictability.

2. **Defensive Attribution Hypothesis** complements this perspective by demonstrating that observers blame victims to reduce their perceived vulnerability to similar misfortunes, effectively creating psychological distance between themselves and the victim.

3. **Fundamental Attribution Error** contributes to victim blaming through the tendency to overemphasize dispositional factors (character flaws, personal choices) while underemphasizing situational constraints when evaluating others' misfortunes.

4. **Social Identity Theory** explains how ingroup/outgroup dynamics influence victim blaming, with observers showing greater empathy and less blame toward similar others while increasing blame toward dissimilar victims.

5. **Empirical evidence** across multiple domains—including sexual assault, cyberbullying, workplace mistreatment, and intimate partner violence—consistently demonstrates that third-party victim blaming reduces helping behavior, discourages victim disclosure, and perpetuates harmful social norms.

6. **Recent research** identifies additional mechanisms such as DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender) tactics, moral disengagement, and counterfactual thinking that further explain how victim blaming operates in social contexts.

7. **Intervention strategies** show promise in reducing victim blaming through education about psychological mechanisms, perspective-taking exercises, and challenging just-world beliefs.

## Findings

### Theoretical Foundations

#### Just World Hypothesis

The Just World Hypothesis, first articulated by Melvin J. Lerner in the 1960s, posits that individuals have a fundamental need to believe that the world is a just and orderly place where people generally receive what they deserve and deserve what they receive. This belief serves as a powerful motivational force behind victim blaming, as attributing fault to victims allows observers to maintain their sense of control and predictability, thereby alleviating the psychological distress that arises from confronting undeserved suffering.

Lerner's foundational work demonstrated that when observers witness innocent victims experiencing misfortune, they experience cognitive dissonance between their belief in a just world and the observed injustice. To resolve this dissonance, observers often blame the victim, reasoning that the victim must have done something to deserve their fate. This mechanism operates largely at an unconscious level and serves as a psychological coping strategy.

Key empirical support includes Lerner and Simmons' 1966 study on observer reactions to innocent victims, which found that participants who witnessed an innocent person receiving electric shocks subsequently derogated the victim to restore their belief in justice. Subsequent research has replicated these findings across diverse contexts, including poverty, illness, accidents, and criminal victimization.

The Just World Hypothesis explains victim blaming as a means of preserving one's belief in a just world, where misfortune is perceived as deserved rather than random. This belief system provides comfort and a sense of control, as it implies that one can avoid victimization by behaving correctly.

#### Defensive Attribution Hypothesis

The Defensive Attribution Hypothesis, developed by Walster (1966) and Shaver (1970), provides a complementary perspective by focusing on observers' need to protect themselves from perceived vulnerability. According to this theory, when observers witness a victim's misfortune, they experience anxiety about their own potential victimization. To reduce this anxiety, they attribute responsibility to the victim, thereby creating psychological distance and reassuring themselves that they can avoid a similar fate by being different from the victim.

Burger's 1981 meta-analysis of the Defensive Attribution Hypothesis provided robust empirical support, finding that observers consistently blame victims more when the victim's misfortune appears random or uncontrollable. The meta-analysis demonstrated that this effect is particularly strong when observers perceive similarity to the victim, as the threat feels more immediate and personal.

The defensive attribution process involves several steps: (1) observers perceive a victim's misfortune, (2) they experience anxiety about their own vulnerability, (3) they attribute responsibility to the victim to reduce this anxiety, and (4) they feel protected by the belief that they can avoid the victim's fate through different behaviors.

Observers may blame victims to reduce their own perceived susceptibility to experience a similar difficulty, effectively using victim blaming as a psychological defense mechanism to distance themselves from the victim and reduce their perceived vulnerability.

#### Fundamental Attribution Error

The Fundamental Attribution Error, identified by Lee Ross and colleagues, describes the tendency for observers to overemphasize dispositional or personality-based explanations for others' behaviors while underemphasizing situational explanations. In the context of victim blaming, this cognitive bias leads third parties to focus on the victim's character, choices, or behaviors while neglecting the situational constraints, environmental factors, or perpetrator actions that contributed to the victimization.

This attribution bias is particularly problematic in victim blaming because it leads observers to conclude that victims are responsible for their misfortune due to inherent flaws or poor decisions, rather than recognizing the complex interplay of factors that often contribute to victimization. The Fundamental Attribution Error operates in conjunction with other mechanisms, amplifying the tendency to blame victims.

Beyond the just-world hypothesis, victim blaming also intersects significantly with attribution theory, particularly the fundamental attribution error, which causes individuals to overemphasize dispositional explanations for behaviors observed in others while underemphasizing situational explanations.

#### Social Identity Theory

Social Identity Theory, developed by Tajfel and Turner, explains how group membership and social categorization influence victim blaming. According to this framework, individuals categorize themselves and others into ingroups (groups they belong to) and outgroups (groups they do not belong to). This categorization affects empathy, attribution of responsibility, and helping behavior.

Research demonstrates that observers show greater empathy and less blame toward victims who are perceived as similar to themselves or as part of their ingroup. Conversely, victims perceived as dissimilar or as part of an outgroup receive more blame and less empathy. This pattern helps explain why victim blaming often occurs along lines of gender, race, ethnicity, and other social identities.

The social identity perspective also accounts for the role of interpersonal distance in victim blaming. Studies have found that victims with closer interpersonal distance to observers receive less blame and more help, consistent with Social Identity Theory's predictions about ingroup favoritism and outgroup derogation.

Compared to victims who have a farther interpersonal distance from bystanders, those with a closer interpersonal distance receive less blame for their negative self-disclosures and receive more help from bystanders. These results validated the social identity theory.

### Cognitive and Motivational Mechanisms

#### Cognitive Dissonance Reduction

Cognitive dissonance theory provides a mechanism for understanding why observers engage in victim blaming. When individuals encounter information that contradicts their existing beliefs (such as the belief in a just world), they experience psychological discomfort. Victim blaming serves to reduce this dissonance by reconciling the observation of victimization with the belief in justice.

This mechanism is particularly strong when the victimization is severe, the victim appears innocent, and the observer has a strong investment in believing in a just world. The more threatening the dissonance, the more intense the victim blaming may become as a means of restoring cognitive consistency.

Victim blaming is assumed to have a strong motivational basis, fueled by the inclination to reduce the dissonance between just world beliefs and victimization of innocent targets.

#### Moral Disengagement

Albert Bandura's theory of moral disengagement explains how individuals can engage in harmful behaviors, including victim blaming, without experiencing moral self-condemnation. Moral disengagement involves cognitive restructuring that allows observers to disengage their moral standards from their actions, making it easier to blame victims and avoid helping.

Mechanisms of moral disengagement relevant to victim blaming include:
- **Moral justification**: Framing victim blaming as serving a higher moral purpose
- **Euphemistic labeling**: Using language that minimizes the harm of blaming
- **Advantageous comparison**: Comparing the victim to others who are perceived as more deserving of blame
- **Displacement of responsibility**: Attributing responsibility for blaming to others or to the situation
- **Diffusion of responsibility**: Spreading blame among multiple parties
- **Distortion of consequences**: Minimizing the harmful effects of victim blaming
- **Dehumanization**: Viewing victims as less than human or deserving of their fate
- **Attribution of blame**: Directly blaming the victim for their misfortune

These mechanisms allow third parties to engage in victim blaming while maintaining a positive self-image and avoiding the guilt that would otherwise accompany such behavior.

#### Counterfactual Thinking

Counterfactual thinking involves the mental simulation of alternatives to past events, often taking the form of "if only" or "what if" statements. In the context of victim blaming, observers engage in upward counterfactuals that focus on how the victim could have avoided their misfortune by acting differently.

This cognitive process leads observers to identify specific actions the victim could have taken to prevent their victimization, thereby increasing attributions of responsibility to the victim. Counterfactual thinking is particularly prevalent when the victim's actions are seen as controllable and when alternative courses of action are easily imaginable.

Thus, the question is: What are the factors that contribute to bystander-blaming? As no research exists on bystander-blaming, this chapter proposes borrowing the theoretical framework of victim-blaming, including the Belief in a Just World Theory, the Defensive Attribution Theory and Counterfactual Thinking.

#### DARVO and Victim Framing

DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender) represents a rhetorical strategy used by perpetrators when confronted with their harmful actions. The sequence involves: (1) Denying the wrongdoing, (2) Attacking the victim or accuser, and (3) Reversing the roles to portray themselves as the true victim.

Research demonstrates that exposure to DARVO tactics significantly influences third-party perceptions. When observers are exposed to DARVO, they judge the perpetrator as less abusive and less responsible, while perceiving the victim as less believable and more blameworthy. This effect operates through cognitive shortcuts and cultural narratives that can be interrupted through education about DARVO mechanisms.

Experimental vignette research deepens this narrative: when observers are exposed to perpetrator DARVO, they judge the perpetrator as less abusive and less responsible, while perceiving the victim as less believable and more blameworthy.

Research suggests that DARVO may be effective at shaping attitudes: exposure to DARVO tactics is associated with increased self-blame by victims and reduced perceptions of victim believability by observers. Fortunately, cultivating awareness of DARVO has been shown to diminish its impact.

### Empirical Evidence Across Contexts

#### Sexual Assault and Intimate Partner Violence

Extensive research in the domain of sexual assault and intimate partner violence has documented pervasive victim blaming by third parties. Studies consistently find that:

- Observers attribute more blame to victims when they engage in behaviors that deviate from traditional gender norms or expectations
- Men are consistently more prone to victim blaming than women across multiple studies
- Victim blaming reduces the likelihood of bystander intervention and support for victims
- Observers often discount the role of perpetrators while overemphasizing victim characteristics or behaviors

Results found that observers discounted the role of the perpetrator in the act of violence and placed more blame on the victim when the victim was verbally aggressive prior to the assault. Studies have found that the observer's gender greatly influences perceptions of rape victims. Men are consistently more prone to victim blaming compared to women.

#### Cyberbullying and Online Contexts

Research on cyberbullying reveals that victim blaming by bystanders is a significant barrier to intervention. Studies demonstrate that:

- Victims who post negative comments online may trigger blame from bystanders, reducing the likelihood of positive intervention
- Interpersonal distance moderates the effect of victim self-disclosure on blaming, with closer relationships leading to less blame
- Belief in a just world predicts victim blaming in online contexts, as observers seek to explain why victims experience cyberbullying

According to belief in a just world theory, individuals tend to believe the world is just and that misfortunes occur due to one's inappropriate actions. The results also offer a psychological perspective explanation of the prevalent and misguided victim-blaming phenomenon observed on current social media platforms.

#### Workplace Mistreatment

In organizational contexts, third-party victim blaming affects both victims and the broader workplace climate:

- Observers of workplace mistreatment often experience strong emotional reactions, including both empathy for victims and victim blaming
- Victim blaming in workplace contexts can create a climate that discourages reporting and help-seeking
- Schadenfreude (pleasure derived from others' misfortune) may coexist with victim blaming in some observers
- Perspective-taking interventions show limited effectiveness in reducing victim blaming in work-related situations

On the encouraging side, we found that observers tend to judge perpetrators and feel empathy for victims. These reactions discourage mistreatment by creating a climate that favors the victim. On the other hand, we found that observers may also enjoy seeing their co-workers suffer or blame the victim.

According to the just-world theory, people tend to blame innocent victims for the misfortune they experienced to preserve the belief in a just world. Our preregistered experimental study demonstrates that perspective-taking does not reduce victim blaming in work-related situations.

#### Social Exclusion and General Contexts

Research on social exclusion and other general contexts provides additional insights:

- Victim blaming in social exclusion situations is motivated by the need to reduce dissonance between just-world beliefs and the observation of innocent suffering
- Similarity between observers and victims reduces blame, as observers are more likely to identify with similar others
- The motivational basis of victim blaming appears strong across diverse contexts, suggesting it is a fundamental social cognitive process

Victim blaming is assumed to have a strong motivational basis, fueled by the inclination to reduce the dissonance between just world beliefs and victimization of innocent targets. Who is blamed for social exclusion often depends on how justified observers perceive the exclusion. Here we hypothesize that observers' own similarity to the excluded person powerfully colors these judgments.

### Moderating Factors

#### Victim Characteristics

Several victim characteristics influence the degree of third-party victim blaming:

- **Perceived similarity**: Victims who are similar to observers receive less blame
- **Identifiability**: Identifiable victims may receive more or less blame depending on context
- **Behavior prior to victimization**: Victims who engaged in non-normative or risky behaviors receive more blame
- **Gender and social identity**: Victims from outgroups or who challenge social norms receive more blame
- **Self-disclosure**: Victims who disclose negative information may trigger more blame from bystanders

Victims posting negative comments online might trigger blame from bystanders, subsequently diminishing their likelihood of positive intervening. Compared to victims who have a farther interpersonal distance from bystanders, those with a closer interpersonal distance receive less blame.
